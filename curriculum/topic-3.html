Topic 3 · Fractures I: The Wrist

Distal radius · scaphoid · carpal fractures · perilunate injuries

The distal radius is the most-tested fracture on the OITE hand section (6% of all hand questions) and the most common operative case in practice. The scaphoid is the most commonly missed carpal injury, and a missed scaphoid becomes a nonunion and then a SNAC wrist.

Interns own: reduction and splinting of a distal radius fracture in the ED, the acceptable-parameters numbers, and never clearing a tender snuffbox on a negative film.

Test yourself

  1. State the acceptable reduction parameters for a distal radius fracture, and what makes one unstable on day-1 films.
  2. A 62-year-old independent, right-hand-dominant woman has an intra-articular distal radius fracture with 3 mm step-off and 25 degrees dorsal tilt. Your recommendation, and what did the WRIST trial show for patients over 60?
  3. Why do proximal-pole scaphoid fractures behave differently from distal-pole? Which patterns can go in a cast and which get a screw?
  4. Snuffbox tenderness with normal films 4 days after a fall: sensitivity of the initial x-ray, and your two acceptable pathways.
  5. Eight weeks after a nondisplaced distal radius fracture the patient suddenly cannot lift the thumb off the table. Diagnosis and treatment?
  6. A high-energy fall labeled “wrist sprain”: the capitate is not seated in the lunate cup on the lateral. Name the injury, the Mayfield sequence, and tonight’s plan.
  7. What fracture follows a direct blow to the hypothenar palm, what view shows it, and what is the classic complication?
Answers
  1. Radial height within 2-3 mm (normal ~11), inclination at least 15 degrees (normal ~22), volar tilt from neutral to 10 dorsal (normal ~11 volar), articular step 2 mm or less, DRUJ congruent. Unstable: dorsal comminution, initial tilt over 20 degrees, intra-articular extension, associated ulnar/DRUJ injury, age.
  2. ORIF with a volar locking plate: step-off over 2 mm plus 25 degrees of tilt in an active, dominant-hand patient. WRIST trial: in patients 60 and over, nonoperative care matched operative fixation on 1-year patient-rated outcomes, so casting is defensible for the low-demand elderly; her independence argues for surgery.
  3. Blood flows retrograde from the dorsal ridge, so the proximal pole is end-vascular: highest nonunion/AVN risk, essentially always operative. Nondisplaced waist and distal pole/tubercle heal in a cast (8-12 weeks; tubercle shorter). Screw for displacement 1 mm or more, proximal pole, or the patient who cannot afford months of immobilization.
  4. Initial films are ~70-80% sensitive. Either thumb spica and repeat imaging at 10-14 days, or early MRI (near 100% sensitive); go straight to MRI for laborers and athletes. Missed scaphoid → nonunion → SNAC.
  5. Attritional EPL rupture at Lister’s tubercle. Direct repair is not possible; EIP-to-EPL transfer.
  6. Perilunate dislocation. Mayfield I-IV: scapholunate, capitolunate, lunotriquetral, then lunate dislocation (“spilled teapot”). Tonight: closed reduction with traction; median nerve symptoms are common, and an acute carpal tunnel picture means emergent release. Definitive: open reduction, ligament repair, K-wires; roughly 25% are missed at first presentation.
  7. Hook of hamate fracture; carpal tunnel view or CT. Classic complications: nonunion with persistent grip pain and small-finger flexor tendon rupture (FDP small sawing on the fracture edge); ulnar nerve symptoms in Guyon’s canal. Treatment for the symptomatic nonunion is excision.
