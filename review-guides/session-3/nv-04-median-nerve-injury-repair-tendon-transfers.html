Median Nerve Injury, Repair, and Tendon Transfers

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • High median palsy (proximal to AIN): loss of FCR, FDS, FDP index (±long), FPL, PQ, thenar muscles, lumbricals 1-2 → index finger rests in full extension, thumb loses opposition + IP flexion
  • Low median palsy (distal to AIN): thenar muscles + lumbricals 1-2 only → normal digital cascade, thenar wasting, lost opposition; clawing rare (interossei compensate)
  • Wrist-level injury: primarily sensory (thumb through radial ring); palmar cutaneous branch spared; thenar wasting
  • Long finger FDP often has dual ulnar + median innervation → may retain flexion
  • Deep head FPB often dual innervated → some palmar abduction preserved even in median palsy

Workup

Exam

  • Sensory: thumb, index, long, radial ring (volar + dorsal tips); palmar cutaneous branch → thenar eminence + radial palm (high vs low localization)
  • Motor: test opposition (APB), FPL (thumb IP flexion), FDP index (DIP flexion), pronation
  • Resting cascade: high palsy = index (±long) in full extension; low palsy = normal cascade + thenar wasting

Electrodiagnostics

  • Wait ≥ 3 weeks post-injury; NCS/EMG distinguishes neurapraxia from axonotmetic injury
  • Cannot reliably differentiate Sunderland II from III/IV early
  • Intraoperative NAP across neuroma-in-continuity: positive = 90% continued recovery

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Neurapraxic/axonotmetic with evidence of recovery: observation + passive ROM
  • Opponens splint (thumb in palmar abduction, slight pronation, slight MP flexion)
  • Buddy straps for index/long finger flexion deficit
  • Consider non-operative if minimal functional deficit or patient has adapted well (e.g., strong pulp-to-side pinch in quadriplegia)

Nerve Repair

  • Same principles as NV-01: resect to healthy fascicles, tension-free, fascicular alignment, epineurial suture
  • Avulsion/blast: wait 2-3 weeks for demarcation; cable graft for gaps
  • Sural nerve = workhorse donor; 30-40 cm per leg; cable graft technique for larger nerves

Nerve Transfers for Motor

  • ECRB branch → AIN: restores extrinsic finger/thumb flexion (FDP index, FPL, PQ)

  • Brachialis branch (musculocutaneous) → AIN or PT: for high injuries

  • ECRB → PT nerve: restores pronation (simultaneous wrist extension + pronation = intuitive re-education)

  • ADM ulnar motor branch → recurrent median motor branch: restores opposition (El-Haj)

  • Nerve transfer technique pearl: check with anesthesia pre-op about avoiding long-acting paralytics — intraoperative stimulation required. At 0.5 mA the donor should fire strongly; at 2.0 mA the recipient target should have zero response. “Donor distal, recipient proximal” for tension-free coaptation.

Nerve Transfers for Sensation

  • Critical: restore sensation to radial index + ulnar thumb for precision pinch
  • Ulnar 4th web space → median 1st web space (end-to-end, with reverse end-to-side for donor recovery)
  • Dorsal cutaneous ulnar → median 1st/2nd web space fascicles (forearm level)
  • Radial sensory branches → ulnar digital thumb + radial digital index (dorsal-to-palmar transfer)
  • FDMA flap (innervated by dorsoradial sensory nerve) for thumb pulp resurfacing — sensate but donor morbidity limits use

Tendon Transfers — Opposition

EIP Opponensplasty (Burkhalter) — preferred for trauma/combined median-ulnar palsy - EIP harvested at index MCP (ulnar side of sagittal band); rerouted dorsal-ulnar → palmar-radial around ulna; Pulvertaft weave into APB insertion - No pulley needed (ulna redirects); radial-nerve innervated (independent of median/ulnar) - Drawbacks: dorsal ulnar sensory nerve at risk; crosses over carpal tunnel scar

FDS Ring Opponensplasty — low median palsy only - FDS harvested through palmar incision; redirected through FCU pulley or Guyon canal/palmar fascia; subcutaneous tunnel across palm → APB insertion - Excellent excursion; all palmar incisions - Drawbacks: swan neck risk (hypermobile patients); median-innervated donor (unavailable in high palsy or combined injury)

ADQ (Huber) Opponensplasty — also for congenital thumb hypoplasia - Ulnar-innervated; restores thenar esthetic; unique cosmetic advantage - Line of pull favors flexion/pronation > abduction - Drawbacks: limited excursion; pedicle at risk; compresses Guyon canal

Camitz (PL → APB) — quick, done with open CTR - Primarily provides abduction only (not true opposition) - PL absent in up to 20%; cannot use in high median palsy

Tendon Transfers — Finger Flexion (High Palsy)

  • FDP “side-car”: paralyzed index (±long) FDP woven into functioning ring/small FDP; restores cascade + IP flexion; loses independent index flexion
  • Alternative: ECRL → index FDP in combined median-ulnar palsy

Tendon Transfers — Thumb IP Flexion (High Palsy)

  • BR → FPL or ECRB → FPL: tension set with wrist neutral, thumb IP/MP at ~30° flexion
  • BR must be extensively freed from radius for adequate excursion
  • Alternative: thumb IP fusion

Post-op / Rehab

  • All opponensplasties: thumb spica 4-6 weeks (thumb palmarly abducted, flexed, pronated, wrist neutral); therapy at 4-6 wk; strengthening at 8 wk
  • FDP side-car: dorsal blocking splint (digits slightly flexed, wrist neutral) x 4-6 weeks
  • Nerve transfers: motor re-education starts at 3 weeks (donor activation, mirror therapy, bilateral exercises)

Complications & Outcomes

  • Nerve transfer outcomes: ECRB→AIN achieves M3-M4 finger/thumb flexion at 12-24 months; brachialis→AIN similar
  • EIP opponensplasty: reliable but EDC subluxation if extensor hood not repaired; EIP absent in 4%
  • FDS opponensplasty: adequate length and excursion; risk of swan neck and friction at pulley site
  • Sural nerve harvest: persistent sensory loss in most (93%); allodynia in 50% but rarely clinically significant
  • Sensory nerve transfer: risk of abnormal sensation, painful donor site, poor cortical reorientation
  • FDP side-car: cannot achieve independent index flexion; transection prevents recovery if nerve regenerates

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Index finger in full extension at rest = pathognomonic for high median palsy
  • Thenar wasting + normal digital cascade = low median palsy
  • EIP opponensplasty = best choice for combined median-ulnar injuries (radial nerve innervated)
  • Camitz = abduction only, not true opposition — quick addition during open CTR in severe CTS with thenar atrophy
  • Don’t forget to test deep head FPB (often ulnar innervated) — some palmar abduction may be preserved
  • Full passive ROM at all joints required before any tendon transfer

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A patient with high median nerve palsy has index and long fingers resting in extension while ring and small fingers maintain normal cascade. What muscles are denervated? — A: FDP to index (AIN), FDS to all digits (median), FPL (AIN), pronator teres, FCR, PQ, thenar muscles, lumbricals 1-2. Ring/small FDP are ulnar-innervated and preserved.
  2. Q: Which opponensplasty is preferred for a patient with combined median and ulnar nerve palsy? — A: EIP (Burkhalter) — it is radial nerve-innervated and does not require a pulley, making it reliable when both median and ulnar motors are unavailable.
  3. Q: What is the preferred nerve transfer to restore forearm pronation in an isolated pronator teres palsy? — A: ECRB branch to PT nerve — synergistic function (wrist extension + pronation occur together) facilitates intuitive motor re-education.
  4. Q: After a high median nerve injury, the long finger retains DIP flexion. Why? — A: Long finger FDP often has dual innervation from both the AIN (median) and ulnar nerve; the ulnar contribution preserves active DIP flexion.
