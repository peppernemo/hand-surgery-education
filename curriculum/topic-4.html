Topic 4 · Fractures II: The Hand

Metacarpal & phalangeal fractures · PIP fracture-dislocations · thumb base · UCL/Stener

Pattern-based decisions: location, angulation, rotation. Rotation is never acceptable; angulation tolerance depends on the digit. The thumb base and the PIP joint are where the traps live.

Interns own: checking and documenting rotation correctly (nails to the scaphoid tubercle in flexion, no scissoring), and the boxer’s fracture conversation.

Test yourself

  1. Acceptable apex-dorsal angulation for a metacarpal neck fracture of each finger, and the finding that is never acceptable.
  2. A 19-year-old punches a wall: small-finger metacarpal neck fracture, 50 degrees, no rotation. The ED wants to reduce and cast. What do you actually do?
  3. Bennett vs Rolando vs reverse Bennett; name the deforming forces on a Bennett and the reduction maneuver.
  4. How does articular percentage guide a dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation, and what are the three surgical options for an unstable one?
  5. Skier with thumb MCP pain: firm endpoint at 20 degrees vs no endpoint at 40 degrees of radial stress. Which goes to the OR and why?
  6. A child has a nailbed injury with a fracture through the distal phalanx physis and the nail plate sitting on top of the eponychial fold. Name the injury and the mistake to avoid.
  7. When is a “stable” PIP fracture-dislocation treated with an extension-block splint, and why is full-time immobilization almost never right?
Answers
  1. Roughly 10-15 degrees index, 15-20 middle, 30-40 ring, 40-50 small (CMC mobility compensates ulnarly). Any rotational deformity is a surgical indication. Dr. Barrera’s practice for the classic boxer’s fracture without rotation: up to ~70 degrees gets buddy taping and early motion (RCT-supported, equivalent outcomes to reduction and casting).
  2. Confirm no rotation with the fingers flexed. Then buddy tape and move early; the reduction rarely holds anyway and the outcome is the same. Counsel honestly about the flatter knuckle with normal function. Operate for rotation, open injury/fight-bite overlay, multiple metacarpals, or an equivalent fracture in the index/middle.
  3. Bennett: two-part intra-articular thumb metacarpal base; the volar-ulnar beak stays (anterior oblique ligament) while APL pulls the shaft proximal-radial and adductor pollicis adducts and supinates. Reduce with traction, pronation, and palmar-abduction pressure; CRPP, or ORIF for a fragment over ~25-30% of the joint. Rolando: comminuted Y/T pattern, worse prognosis. Reverse Bennett: fifth metacarpal base, shaft pulled by ECU.
  4. Under 30-40%: usually stable after reduction, extension-block splint and early motion. 30-50%: tenuous. Over 50%: unstable. Options: hemi-hamate autograft (modern choice for acute 30-60% single-fragment), volar plate arthroplasty, dynamic external fixation for comminuted pilon patterns. The real test is stability through the arc on lateral fluoro.
  5. No endpoint at 40 degrees = complete UCL tear, likely Stener lesion (adductor aponeurosis interposed; it cannot heal against the ligament) → open repair. Firm endpoint at 20 degrees = partial tear → thumb spica brace.
  6. Seymour fracture: open physeal fracture with interposed nailbed. The mistake is treating it as a simple nailbed injury: it needs debridement, removal of interposed matrix, reduction, antibiotics; missed Seymours become osteomyelitis and growth arrest.
  7. Extension-block when the joint is congruent and stable through the allowed arc: it keeps the joint moving while the volar lip heals. Full-time immobilization trades a reduced joint for a stiff one; the PIP is a stiffness machine, and motion is the outcome.
