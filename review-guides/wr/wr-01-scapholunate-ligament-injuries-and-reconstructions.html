Scapholunate Ligament Injuries and Reconstructions

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • SLIL has 3 regions: dorsal (strongest, greatest yield strength), proximal (membranous fibrocartilage), palmar
  • Dorsal SLIL is the primary restraint to translation; dorsal + palmar both resist rotation
  • DCSS (dorsal capsulo-scapholunate septum) — X-shaped crossed fibers linking extrinsic and intrinsic ligaments; critical to the “dorsal SL complex”
  • Tear progression: volar → membranous → dorsal (Mayfield)
  • Dorsal fibers intact → minimal kinematic change; dorsal torn → substantial instability
  • Secondary stabilizers: RSC ligament, scaphocapitate ligament, scaphotrapezial ligaments — attritional failure over time leads to static malalignment
  • End-stage: SLAC arthritis (predictable degenerative pattern: radial styloid → scaphoid fossa → capitolunate → pancarpal)

Classification

  • Geissler arthroscopic grading (I-IV): I = attenuation/hemorrhage, no midcarpal incongruity; II = slight gap (< probe width); III = probe passes through gap; IV = gross instability, 2.7 mm scope passes through
  • Garcia-Elias staging (1-6): progression from partial tear with intact dorsal SL → irreducible malalignment → SLAC arthrosis
  • EWAS/IWAS classification (I-V): pre-dynamic → dynamic → static instability; subdivides stage III into A (volar), B (dorsal), C (complete SLIOL tear, reducible)

Workup

History

  • Any wrist trauma can cause acute SL injury; high association with distal radius fractures and radial styloid fractures
  • Suspect SL injury when fracture line exits near SL interval

Exam

  • Watson scaphoid shift test: thumb on scaphoid tuberosity, wrist from ulnar to radial deviation → dorsal clunk with release; painful clunk = pathologic (present in 25-32% of asymptomatic wrists bilaterally)
  • Positive only if dorsal SL ligament is completely disrupted
  • Dorsal pain during the maneuver is of primary importance

Imaging

  • PA/lateral + grip views: Terry Thomas sign (SL gap > 3 mm pathologic, > 5 mm definitive), scaphoid ring sign (foreshortened flexed scaphoid), disrupted Gilula’s lines, triangular lunate
  • Lateral: SL angle > 60° = SL dissociation; DISI pattern
  • Carpal height index (proximal lunate → distal capitate / 3rd MC length) quantifies collapse
  • MRI: sensitivity 0.70, specificity 0.90 — cannot reliably exclude intrinsic ligament tears
  • Arthroscopy remains gold standard for diagnosis, staging, and treatment planning

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Most SL tears never reach surgical care
  • Garcia-Elias selective muscle re-education: activate ECRL, APL, FCR (SL-friendly muscles); inhibit ECU
  • Dart-thrower’s motion rehabilitation (minimal SL movement through this arc)
  • Best for IWAS stage I; may temporize in some stage II injuries

Operative

  • Healing potential diminishes with time: acute (best), subacute 3-6 wk (reduced), chronic > 6 wk (minimal primary healing)
  • Treatment by IWAS stage:
    • Stage I: PRP or physical therapy; ADCLR if refractory
    • Stage II (A/B/C): ADCLR (arthroscopic dorsal capsuloligamentous repair) ± AVCLR if persistent volar instability
    • Stage III: wide ADCLR ± AVCLR
    • Stage IV: wide ADCLR or ligament reconstruction with tendon graft/synthetic bracing
    • Stage V (DISI): 360° arthroscopic ligamentoplasty or open ligamentoplasty (modified Brunelli), BTB repair
  • K-wire fixation: SL + SC ± LT pins; maintain 6-8 weeks for soft tissue healing
  • Chronic without arthritis: same goal of restoring dorsal SL complex anatomy via ADCLR or reconstruction
  • SLAC wrist (Garcia-Elias stage 6): salvage — PRC, 4-corner arthrodesis, total wrist arthrodesis

Complications & Outcomes

  • Arthroscopic techniques show excellent early/midterm results vs historical open methods, but no RCTs
  • Open capsulodesis: associated with ROM loss
  • Tendon reconstruction: difficulty balancing tendon length; multiple techniques exist because none is clearly superior
  • Fusions (intercarpal or wrist) eliminate normal kinematics and may lead to adjacent arthrosis

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Dorsal SL complex = SLIL + DCSS + extrinsic ligaments — all must be addressed for stable repair
  • Always check for secondary stabilizer insufficiency (RSC, scaphocapitate ligaments)
  • Watson test has high false-positive rate — correlate with pain and asymmetry
  • Grip views and dynamic fluoroscopy can unmask dynamic instability missed on static films
  • SL angle > 60° on lateral is the key radiographic finding
  • When multiple reconstruction options exist and none is clearly superior, it means the problem is unsolved

Board-Style Questions (3-5)

  1. Q: Which portion of the scapholunate interosseous ligament is the strongest and most important for carpal stability? — A: The dorsal portion (greatest yield strength and primary restraint to translation).
  2. Q: A patient with a positive Watson scaphoid shift test has an SL angle of 72° on lateral radiograph and no arthritis. What stage of Garcia-Elias classification is this? — A: Stage 4 (complete SL tear, not repairable, reducible rotary subluxation, no arthritis).
  3. Q: In the Mayfield progressive perilunar instability model, what is the typical sequence of SLIL tear progression? — A: Volar → membranous → dorsal.
  4. Q: What is the SLAC arthrosis degenerative pattern in order of involvement? — A: Radial styloid-scaphoid → scaphoid fossa → capitolunate → pancarpal (radiolunate usually spared).
  5. Q: What muscle re-education program is recommended for non-operative management of SL instability? — A: Co-contraction of ECRL, APL, and FCR (dart-thrower’s motion muscles) with inhibition of ECU.
