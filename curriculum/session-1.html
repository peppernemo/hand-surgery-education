Session 1 — Hand Exam Fundamentals & Soft-Tissue Emergencies

Week 1 of 8

The systematic hand examination is foundational to all subsequent clinical decision-making. Soft-tissue emergencies — pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, compartment syndrome, high-pressure injection injury, necrotizing fasciitis, and human bite wounds — represent time-sensitive diagnoses encountered most often on overnight consults. Delay in recognition and treatment can result in permanent functional loss or amputation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, the resident should be able to: - Perform a complete hand exam on any consult within 10 minutes - Isolate FDS from FDP, EPL from EIP, and test each intrinsic group individually - Recognize and describe the four Kanavel signs - Identify a compartment syndrome of the forearm/hand and know when to take to OR - Describe the management of a fight bite and a high-pressure injection injury - Differentiate felon, paronychia, herpetic whitlow, and flexor tenosynovitis - State the absolute and relative indications for replantation, including the ischemia window for thumb vs digit and the post-op monitoring regimen

Questions (attempt before viewing answers)

Anatomy & classification

  1. Name the 5 flexor tendon zones and the anatomic landmark that bounds each.
  2. What are the 10 structures that pass through the carpal tunnel?
  3. List the intrinsic muscles of the hand by nerve supply (median vs ulnar).
  4. What is the cutaneous innervation of the hand — which nerve supplies the radial dorsum, the palmar thumb–radial-ring, the dorsal small finger, and the palmar ring–small?

Exam maneuvers

  1. How do you isolate FDS from FDP on exam? What’s the pitfall with the small finger, and what does it mean if the patient can’t flex the FDS of the small finger in isolation?
  2. A patient cannot retropulse (lift) the thumb off the table with the hand placed flat, but can still extend the thumb MCP. Which tendon is out, and what muscle is compensating?
  3. Describe how you test the intrinsics in a patient with a suspected ulnar neuropathy — be specific about finger positioning.

Clinical vignettes

  1. A 32-year-old man comes in 18 hours after punching someone in the mouth and now has a 3 mm laceration over his long-finger MCP with pain on active flexion. What’s the diagnosis, workup, and management?
  2. A painter presents with a pinhole entry wound at the pulp of his index finger from a paint gun 4 hours ago. Fingertip looks pink and he has mild pain. What do you do?
  3. A 45-year-old diabetic with a 3-day history of progressively worsening pain in the index finger after a rose-thorn puncture. On exam the finger is held in slight flexion, is diffusely tender along the flexor sheath, painful with passive extension, and fusiform-swollen. Name the syndrome, the four classic signs, and your management plan.
  4. A 25-year-old sustained a crush to the forearm 6 hours ago. He reports pain out of proportion, and his pain is worse with passive finger extension. Compartments feel tense. What’s the next step? At what compartment pressure (or Δ pressure) do you take to OR?

Board-style (OITE)

Soft-tissue emergencies — mixed

  1. Differentiate felon, paronychia, and herpetic whitlow — location, etiology, and the single most important management decision for each (especially the one you should NOT I&D).
  2. A patient presents with rapidly spreading erythema, dusky skin, crepitus, and a soft-tissue gas pattern on x-ray. LRINEC score is 9. What is your next step, and what is the role of the ED vs OR vs ICU here?

Replantation

  1. A 28-year-old factory worker sustains a clean-cut amputation of the right dominant thumb at the MCP level 2 hours ago. Cold ischemia time. What are your indications for replantation, what’s your acceptable ischemia window, and what’s the post-op monitoring regimen?
Answer Key (open after attempting all questions)

  1. Flexor zones:

    • Zone I: distal to FDS insertion on middle phalanx — only FDP
    • Zone II: FDS insertion to distal palmar crease (A1 pulley) — “no man’s land”; both FDS + FDP
    • Zone III: distal palmar crease to distal edge of transverse carpal ligament — lumbricals originate here
    • Zone IV: within the carpal tunnel
    • Zone V: proximal to the carpal tunnel (forearm)

  2. Carpal tunnel contents: 4 FDS + 4 FDP + 1 FPL + median nerve = 10 structures. FCR sits in its own sheath outside the canal. Palmaris longus, ulnar nerve/artery, and radial artery are all outside.

  3. Intrinsics by nerve:

    • Median (recurrent motor branch): APB, opponens pollicis, FPB superficial head, lateral 2 lumbricals (index + middle)
    • Ulnar (deep motor branch): all other intrinsics — hypothenar (ADM, FDM brevis, opponens digiti minimi), all 4 dorsal + 3 palmar interossei, medial 2 lumbricals (ring + small), adductor pollicis, FPB deep head

  4. Cutaneous innervation:

    • Radial dorsum: superficial radial nerve
    • Palmar thumb–index–long–radial half of ring: median
    • Dorsal small + dorsal ulnar ring: dorsal cutaneous branch of ulnar
    • Palmar ring (ulnar half) + small: ulnar

  5. FDS vs FDP isolation: Hold all non-tested fingers in full extension (blocks FDP via common muscle belly); patient flexes the PIP of the test finger. Pitfall: ~15-20% of people lack an independent FDS to the small finger (common FDS belly) → “negative” FDS test in the small finger alone is not evidence of injury. Always test the small finger alongside its neighbors first.

  6. EPL rupture, EPB compensating. EPL retropulses the thumb off a flat table and extends the thumb IP — both lost when the EPL is out. EPB extends the thumb MCP, which is why the patient can still extend the MCP. (EIP does not act on the thumb at all — it extends the index finger.) Classic mechanism: attritional rupture at Lister’s tubercle after a non-displaced distal radius fracture.

  7. Intrinsic testing:

    • Froment’s: pinch a paper between thumb + index — positive = IP flexion (FPL substituting for weak adductor pollicis)
    • Wartenberg’s sign: small finger held abducted (unopposed EDM without third palmar interosseous)
    • Crossed fingers (index over long): tests first dorsal + second palmar interossei
    • Card test: abduction/adduction of fingers against resistance

  8. Fight bite. Goes to OR for formal I&D regardless of how small the laceration looks — the tooth penetrates the extensor hood and MCP joint capsule with the finger in flexion, then the injury “retracts” to a different anatomic level at rest. Workup: x-ray (tooth fragments, fx, air), tetanus, rabies assessment (human only), broad empiric abx (amp-sulbactam covers Eikenella). OR for arthrotomy, I&D, capsule repair. IV abx, admit.

  9. High-pressure injection. Surgical emergency even if the wound looks benign. Delay to debridement > 6 hours dramatically increases amputation rate (~50% → 80%+). Paint and diesel are the worst. OR for extensile debridement, serial washouts, open wounds, IV abx, delayed closure or flap. Counsel the patient about amputation risk up front.

  10. Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis. Kanavel’s four signs:

    1. Fusiform (sausage) swelling of the finger
    2. Finger held in slight flexion
    3. Tenderness along the flexor sheath
    4. Pain with passive extension (most specific) Management: IV abx (vanc + cefepime empirically; adjust to cultures), OR for sheath I&D. Gold-standard: limited open incisions proximal (A1) and distal (distal to A4 / DIP) with catheter irrigation of the sheath. Staph (including MRSA) and Strep most common; Neisseria gonorrhoeae in sexually active young adults.

  11. Compartment syndrome, take to OR. Absolute threshold: Δ pressure (diastolic BP − compartment pressure) < 30 mmHg, OR absolute compartment pressure > 30-45 mmHg. Clinical diagnosis wins over numbers — pain out of proportion + pain with passive stretch is enough. Fasciotomy: volar forearm (Henry or McConnell), dorsal forearm, mobile wad, +/- carpal tunnel release, hand (2 dorsal incisions for interossei, thenar + hypothenar releases).

  12. c. Durkan’s compression test — ~87% sensitivity, most sensitive among the three physical exam maneuvers. Thenar atrophy is late/specific but not sensitive.

  13. b. Anterior interosseous nerve — AIN supplies FPL, FDP to index and long, and pronator quadratus. Inability to make an OK sign = loss of IP flexion of the thumb (FPL) and DIP flexion of the index (FDP to index).

  14. Felon / paronychia / herpetic whitlow:

    • Felon: pulp-space abscess (fibrous septa trap pus). I&D through a lateral (or distal volar midline) incision, avoiding the digital nerve. Do NOT fishmouth.
    • Paronychia: infection at the eponychial/lateral nail fold. I&D by lifting the nail fold or partial nail removal. Conservative management (warm soaks, oral abx) if no frank abscess.
    • Herpetic whitlow: viral (HSV). Do NOT incise — risk of bacterial superinfection and prolonged shedding. Acyclovir, dry dressing, self-limited.

  15. Necrotizing fasciitis. LRINEC ≥ 8 has high positive predictive value. Emergency broad-spectrum abx (vanc + pip-tazo + clinda for toxin suppression), aggressive resuscitation, immediate OR for wide debridement. Do not wait for imaging to confirm. The diagnosis is clinical and surgical. Expect serial debridements every 24-48 h.

  16. Thumb replant.

    • Absolute indications: thumb (any level), multiple digits, transmetacarpal, partial hand, any pediatric amputation, wrist and forearm (sharp mechanism)
    • Relative: single digit distal to FDS insertion (zone I), ring avulsion type 3
    • Relative contraindications: single digit in zone II (proximal to FDS), severe crush/avulsion, multi-level injury, medical comorbidity, prolonged warm ischemia (> 6 h digit, > 12 h with cooling; muscle-containing parts > 6 h warm / 12 h cold)
    • This case (dominant thumb, clean cut, 2 h cold): replant.
    • Post-op monitoring: q1h flap checks (color, cap refill, turgor, Doppler), warm room, dangle protocol, aspirin, +/- heparin, avoid nicotine/caffeine/cold/constricting bandages, leech therapy if venous congestion. Watch for arterial (pale, slow cap refill) vs venous (dusky, brisk cap refill) compromise — OR for re-exploration.
