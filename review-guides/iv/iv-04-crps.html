Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Persistent extremity pain beyond cellular injury or nerve distribution
  • Type I (RSD): no identifiable nerve lesion
  • Type II (Causalgia): identifiable nerve lesion
  • Pain pathway: transduction → A-delta (small myelinated) + C-fiber (unmyelinated) → dorsal horn → spinothalamic tract → cortex
  • Peripheral sensitization: substance P, bradykinin, increased ectopic alpha-adrenergic receptors
  • Cortical reorganization of somatotopic maps (Maihofner) — central component
  • AV shunting → nutritional deprivation of tissues
  • Inflammatory mediators: TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-2, IL-6

SMP vs SIP

  • Sympathetically maintained pain (SMP): responds to sympatholytic intervention (stellate block, phentolamine)
  • Sympathetically independent pain (SIP): does not respond
  • Warm subtype (acute) vs cold subtype (chronic — worse prognosis)

Budapest Criteria (2010, Validated)

  1. Continuing pain disproportionate to inciting event
  2. ≥ 1 symptom in ≥ 3 of 4 categories:
    • Sensory (hyperesthesia/allodynia)
    • Vasomotor (temp asymmetry, skin color)
    • Sudomotor/edema (edema, sweating changes)
    • Motor/trophic (decreased ROM, weakness/tremor/dystonia, skin/hair/nail changes)
  3. ≥ 1 sign at evaluation in ≥ 2 of 4 categories (same list)
  4. No alternative diagnosis

Epidemiology

  • Incidence: 5.5-26.2/100,000; F:M 1.6-4.5:1; mean age 40

  • Type-I has genetic/familial component; 10% have no inciting event

  • 2-39% after distal radius fracture; 8.3% after carpal tunnel release

  • Risk factors: migraine, female, postmenopausal, smoking (68% increased risk), prolonged tourniquet, immobilization

  • Implicated nerves: palmar cutaneous branch median, median, dorsal ulnar, superficial radial, ulnar at elbow, PIN

  • CTR-associated CRPS at 8.3% warrants preop counseling. Risk factors include prior CRPS, psychiatric history, and fibromyalgia. Early recognition is the single best prognostic factor.

Workup

Exam

  • Full upper extremity + cervical + contralateral assessment
  • Pain: burning, throbbing, shooting — out of proportion, non-dermatomal (stocking-glove)
  • Trophic changes: shiny/waxy skin, hair loss, nail changes, pulp atrophy
  • Motor: weakness, tremor, dystonia (10-15% late stage)
  • Vasomotor: asymmetric sweating, flushing, edema
  • Check for adhesive capsulitis (shoulder-hand syndrome)
  • Evaluate nociceptive foci: neuroma, compressive neuropathy, OC fracture
  • Severe swelling → consider factitious behavior

Imaging

  • Sudeck’s atrophy = periarticular osteopenia on x-ray (classic but late finding)
  • Abnormal x-ray in 70-80% (low sensitivity, reasonable specificity)
  • Bone scan: reasonable specificity, low sensitivity — not for routine diagnosis
  • MRI not routinely indicated

Other

  • Labs: CBC, ESR/CRP, HbA1c, autoimmune panel, HIV
  • NCV/EMG to rule out compression/injury
  • Stellate ganglion block (lidocaine): pain relief → SMP; no relief → SIP or alternate dx
  • Continuous brachial plexus block — more reliable diagnostic + therapeutic

Treatment

Prognosis

  • 80% treated within 1st year improve
  • < 50% treated after 1 year improve
  • Cold skin temp = worse prognosis (more likely persistent > 1 yr)
  • Early multidisciplinary management = best outcomes

Hand Therapy

  • Active + passive ROM within pain limits
  • Edema control, contrast baths, desensitization, TENS
  • Graded motor imagery (laterality recognition, mirror therapy, imagined movements) — equivalent/superior to medication in some studies
  • Static + dynamic orthotic positioning

Pharmacologic — Strong Evidence

Agent Dosing
Bisphosphonates (1st-line) Alendronate 40 mg PO daily × 8 wk
Calcitonin 100 U/day SC × 4 wk (perioperative)
Gabapentin 600-1800 mg PO daily × 8 wk
Corticosteroids Prednisolone 30-40 mg/day × 3-4 wk (acute only)
IV Ketamine Subanesthetic (refractory)
Topical DMSO Warm-type
  • Vitamin C: contradictory evidence but “practical merit” (AAOS); 500-1000 mg/day × 50 days; avoid in renal stone history
  • TCAs (amitriptyline 25 tid or 50 qhs), CCBs (nifedipine 10-30 tid), clonidine patch
  • NOT consistently effective: oral opioids, antidepressants per formal recs, oral muscle relaxants

Interventional

  • Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) — strong evidence for pain, QOL, satisfaction
  • Dorsal root ganglion stimulation — newer; cost-effective for refractory
  • Sympathectomy (RF or chemical) — short-term (6-12 wk then receptor upregulation)

Surgical

  • Nerve decompression if compression identified
  • Release contracted joints, intrinsic releases, osteotomy for malunion
  • Surgery in SMP can be safe with continuous sympatholytic protection
  • Realistic goal: 50% improvement

Amputation — Salvage Only

  • Recurrence in stump 43-82%; phantom pain > 70%
  • Indications: chronic infection, ADL-limiting contractures, weeping skin, edema

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Budapest criteria: pain + 3/4 symptom categories + 2/4 sign categories + no alternative
  • Early recognition (< 6 mo) is the single best prognostic factor
  • Bisphosphonates = first-line pharmacotherapy (especially with bone resorption)
  • Graded motor imagery is underutilized and evidence-supported
  • Cold skin = worse prognosis; warm = better response to DMSO
  • Stellate block differentiates SMP from SIP — critical for treatment planning
  • Amputation has 43-82% stump recurrence — use only as salvage
  • Vitamin C prophylaxis is low-risk and reasonable for distal radius fractures

Board-Style Questions

  1. Q: What are the Budapest criteria categories for CRPS diagnosis? A: Sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/edema, motor/trophic — need symptoms in ≥ 3 of 4 and signs in ≥ 2 of 4, plus disproportionate pain and no alternative diagnosis.
  2. Q: What is the incidence of CRPS after distal radius fracture? A: 2-39%; risk factors include age < 60, psychiatric history, prior CRPS.
  3. Q: What is first-line pharmacotherapy for CRPS with bone resorption? A: Bisphosphonates (alendronate 40 mg daily × 8 weeks).
  4. Q: What distinguishes SMP from SIP? A: SMP responds to sympatholytic intervention (stellate block); SIP does not.
  5. Q: What is the classic radiographic finding in CRPS? A: Sudeck’s atrophy — periarticular osteopenia.
