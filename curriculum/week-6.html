Week 6 · Nerve: Injury & Compression

Resident track, week 6 of 7 · Intern track, week 4 of 4 (carpal tunnel portion)

Compressive neuropathy is the highest-volume elective work in hand surgery, and nerve trauma is where exam skill pays off most. This week: Seddon and Sunderland, the CTS-6, cubital tunnel, and telling the mimics apart (AIN vs high median, CTS vs cervical radiculopathy).

Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. Compare Seddon and Sunderland. Which grades recover spontaneously, and at what rate does an axon regenerate?
  2. Walk through the CTS-6: the six items and the cutoff, and what the score means for ordering electrodiagnostics.
  3. Name the five sites of ulnar nerve compression at the elbow, proximal to distal.
  4. Grade cubital tunnel severity (McGowan). When do you operate, and when do you transpose instead of decompress in situ?
  5. How do you distinguish AIN palsy from a high median nerve injury on exam?
  6. Durkan, Phalen, Tinel at the wrist: technique and rough sensitivity of each.
  7. A median nerve repaired at the mid-forearm shows no thenar recovery at 6 months. Why are motor nerve transfers usually off the table by then, and what is the workhorse reconstruction?
Answer key
  1. Neurapraxia (Sunderland I): conduction block, full recovery in weeks. Axonotmesis (II-IV): II recovers by regeneration at ~1 mm/day; III is variable (endoneurial scarring); IV is a neuroma-in-continuity that needs surgery. Neurotmesis (V): transection, always surgical. I-II recover spontaneously, III is variable, IV-V need repair.
  2. Numbness in the median distribution (3.5), nocturnal numbness (4.0), thenar weakness or atrophy (5.0), Phalen positive (5.0), 2PD loss (4.5), Tinel positive (4.0); maximum 26. Score > 12 means CTS is likely (~80%), and with a consistent clinical picture electrodiagnostics are not required before offering release; 12 or less, get EDX.
  3. Arcade of Struthers, medial intermuscular septum, retrocondylar groove at the medial epicondyle, Osborne’s ligament between the FCU heads, deep FCU fascia.
  4. McGowan I (sensory only): night splinting in extension, activity modification. II (weakness without atrophy) and III (atrophy, measurable motor loss): operate. In situ decompression is the modern default; transpose for a subluxing nerve, revision surgery, or structural compression.
  5. AIN palsy has no sensory loss: FPL, index FDP, and pronator quadratus are out (no OK sign) but sensation is intact. A high median lesion adds sensory loss in the radial 3.5 digits plus FDS, FCR, and pronator teres.
  6. Durkan: direct carpal tunnel compression 30 seconds, ~87% sensitive, the best of the three. Phalen: full wrist flexion 60 seconds, ~68%. Tinel: percussion over the nerve, ~50% sensitive but fairly specific.
  7. Motor endplates degenerate by roughly 12-18 months, and a transfer still needs months of regeneration time, so by 6 months without recovery the window is closing; tendon transfer (EIP opponensplasty for thenar function) is the reliable answer. Sensory reconstruction has no such clock.

Run the check-in

Cases to walk through:

  1. ★ A 55-year-old woman has a year of nocturnal numbness in the thumb, index, and middle fingers; Durkan and Phalen positive, early thenar atrophy, CTS-6 of 18. Take her from clinic to the OR: do you order EDX, how do you consent her, open vs endoscopic, and what does recovery look like?
  2. A 60-year-old diabetic has progressive ring and small finger numbness, weak grip, intrinsic atrophy, positive Froment and Wartenberg. Grade it, choose your operation, and defend in situ vs transposition.
  3. A 45-year-old sustained a sharp volar forearm laceration; median motor and sensory are out. Walk through acute repair principles (timing, technique, what “good” looks like under the microscope) and how you will follow recovery month by month.
