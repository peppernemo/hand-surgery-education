Radial Nerve Injury and Tendon Transfers

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Most common peripheral nerve injury with long-bone fractures — 11.8% of humeral shaft fractures
  • Injury at junction of middle/distal thirds of humerus (least mobile segment)
  • Holstein-Lewis (distal humeral shaft) fractures: up to 25% radial nerve palsy
  • 71% of humeral shaft fracture radial palsies resolve with observation
  • Reinnervation order (proximal→distal): BR → ECRL → ECRB → EDC → ECU → EDQ → APL → EPL → EPB → EIP
  • Radial nerve has best recovery prognosis of the three major UE nerves: shorter distance to motor end plates, larger caliber, distinct fascicular organization, motor-predominant with similar target muscles

Workup

Exam

  • High radial palsy: loss of BR, ECRL, ECRB, EDC, ECU, EIP, thumb extensors → no wrist/finger/thumb MCP extension + decreased 2PD dorsal hand
  • Very proximal: also loses triceps + posterior arm sensation
  • PIN palsy: BR + ECRL preserved → wrist extends with radial deviation; SBRN intact
  • MRC grading; donor must be ≥ M4 (loses one grade with transfer)

Electrodiagnostics

  • Begin 6 weeks post-injury; MUPs on EMG = potential for recovery
  • No MUPs at 3 months → explore

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Mainstay for closed palsies; observe 3 months
  • Cock-up wrist orthosis + dynamic outriggers; maintain passive ROM
  • Radial nerve palsy alone is not an indication for surgical fixation of humeral fracture

Nerve Repair/Reconstruction

  • Primary end-to-end: ideal; 91% ≥ M3 with primary suture (Murovic)
  • Tension-free; resect to healthy fascicles; humeral shortening for 1-2 cm gaps
  • Avulsions = universally failed with acute repair (inadequate debridement)
  • Autograft: sural nerve or medial antebrachial cutaneous; Berger: 77% M4-M5 with interfascicular graft (avg 6.6 cm)
  • Gaps > 8 cm = significantly worse outcomes (Petkovic)
  • Open injuries, concomitant vascular injury, no recovery at 3 months → surgical exploration

Nerve Transfers (Median-to-Radial)

  • FDS branch → ECRB (wrist extension)

  • FCR branch → PIN (finger + thumb extension)

  • “Donor distal, recipient proximal” — curvilinear proximal forearm incision

  • Ray & Mackinnon (19 pts): 18/19 M4+ wrist extension at 20 mo; 12/19 M4+ finger/thumb extension

  • Ideal window: 3-6 months from injury; reinnervation goal within 12-18 months

  • Advantages: faster proximal-level recovery, avoids injured field, no graft, independent finger extension

  • 9 patients also received concurrent PT→ECRB tendon transfer as dynamic internal splint

  • Key teaching point: nerve transfers restore independent finger extension and don’t sacrifice wrist flexion power. The trade-off is 9-12 months waiting for reinnervation vs immediate function with tendon transfer.

Tendon Transfers

Principles (7 rules)

  1. Donor must be expendable
  2. Adequate strength (loses 1 grade)
  3. Similar excursion/resting length
  4. One function per tendon
  5. Straight line of pull
  6. Synergistic (wrist flexion = finger extension; wrist extension = finger flexion)
  7. Full passive ROM at all joints before transfer

Timing

  • Observe 3 months after injury; 6-9 months after nerve repair
  • Can be performed decades after injury (Brodman: successful at 24 years)

Standard Transfers for High Radial Palsy

Wrist extension: PT → ECRB (centralized extension) — most common, all three methods use this

Finger + Thumb Extension — 3 Methods:

Method Finger Extension Thumb Extension Key Advantage Key Drawback
FCU-based (Jones) FCU → EDC PL → EPL Historic standard Loses ulnar deviation → radial drift; FCU excursion may be short
FCR-based (Starr/Brand) FCR → EDC PL → EPL Preserves FCU (powerful flexor) FCR excursion may require wrist flexion for full finger extension; needs PL
FDS-based (Boyes) FDS III → EDC EIP → EPL Best excursion match; works when PL absent Loses FDS grip strength; IOM route risks AIN/PIN vessels
  • FCR-based advantage: preserves FCU (most powerful wrist flexor) for a balanced wrist. Trade-off: may need slight wrist flexion for full finger extension. If PL absent, switch to FDS-based method.

Outcomes

  • Moussavi (41 pts): all three methods successful; no ROM/DASH/RTW difference; 95% would repeat; mean RTW 4.5 months (FCR group)
  • Ishida & Ikuta (21 pts, FCR-based, 11.3 yr): avg wrist extension 54°, flexion 42°; MCP extension 5° with wrist at 30° extension; grip 63% contralateral; bowstringing in 12/21 (all PL→EPL)

Triceps Restoration (Rare — Very Proximal Injury)

  • Posterior deltoid → triceps (needs graft to elongate) or biceps → triceps (single-stage, simpler)

Complications & Outcomes

  • Tendon transfer: reliable, time-proven; potential bowstringing (especially PL→EPL), radial deviation (FCU method), stiffness
  • Nerve transfer: less dissection, no strength loss, independent finger extension; but 9-12 month wait
  • Jain 2023 systematic review: TT clinical success 82% vs nerve transfer 59% vs nerve grafts
  • Bertelli 2020: nerve transfers better — TT group had wrist flexion limitation (9/13), permanent radial deviation (5/13), 50% needed wrist flexion for full finger extension

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Radial nerve palsy with closed humeral fracture → observe 3 months; 71% recover spontaneously
  • Open fractures + radial palsy → early exploration (lower spontaneous recovery)
  • PT → ECRB is universal across all three transfer methods — start learning this one
  • Don’t sacrifice FCU if you can avoid it — it’s the most powerful wrist flexor and critical for ulnar deviation
  • PL absence (up to 20%) shifts you from FCR-based to FDS-based method
  • Concurrent nerve transfer + tendon transfer (e.g., FDS→ECRB as “internal splint” while awaiting nerve transfer recovery) is an emerging strategy
  • Full passive ROM mandatory before any tendon transfer

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A patient sustains a closed mid-shaft humerus fracture with complete wrist drop. At 6 weeks, EMG shows no MUPs in radial-innervated muscles. What is the management? — A: Continue observation to 3 months total; 71% of closed fracture radial palsies recover spontaneously. Splint with cock-up orthosis + maintain passive ROM.
  2. Q: In a FCR-based tendon transfer for radial nerve palsy, which tendon powers wrist extension, finger extension, and thumb extension? — A: PT → ECRB (wrist), FCR → EDC (fingers), PL → EPL (thumb).
  3. Q: What is the primary advantage of nerve transfer over tendon transfer for radial nerve palsy? — A: Nerve transfer restores independent finger extension, avoids sacrifice of wrist flexion strength, and avoids the radial deviation deformity seen with FCU-based tendon transfers.
  4. Q: What radial nerve graft gap length is associated with significantly worse outcomes? — A: > 8 cm (Petkovic); for median nerve > 5 cm, for ulnar nerve > 4.5 cm.
