Brachial Plexus Injuries

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Incidence: ~0.6 per 100,000; 1.2% of trauma cases at level-1 centers; male:female 9:1; average age ~30
  • Mechanisms: traffic accidents, ATVs/snowmobiles, penetrating trauma, shoulder impact
  • Upper plexus: traction separating arm from cervical spine or direct supraclavicular impact
  • Lower plexus: sudden hyperabduction or anterior shoulder impact
  • Most common patterns (descending): panplexus > C5-C8 > C5-C6 > C5-C7
  • True 5-level avulsion rare (2-5% of panplexus)
  • Preganglionic (avulsion): cell body in DRG intact → SNAP preserved despite sensory loss (hallmark EDX finding)
  • Postganglionic (rupture): Wallerian degeneration occurs; SNAP lost

Classification

  • Seddon: neurapraxia / axonotmesis / neurotmesis
  • Sunderland: 1st-5th degree (ascending histologic damage)
  • MacKinnon grade VI: mixed pattern across zone of injury

Workup

Exam

  • Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhydrosis) → likely C8-T1 avulsion
  • Scapular winging → proximal upper trunk branch injury (long thoracic / dorsal scapular)
  • Shoulder “ptosis” (sulcus below AC joint) → deltoid paralysis
  • MRC grading 0-5; nearly all function within M4 — reliable serial testing critical
  • Tinel sign: absent = preganglionic; stationary = non-recovering postganglionic (Sunderland IV/V); advancing distally = recovering (Sunderland II/III)
  • Bouvier maneuver to assess intrinsic function
  • Screen for concurrent rotator cuff injury (10% of BPI patients)

Electrodiagnostics

  • Obtain at 4-6 weeks (allow Wallerian degeneration)
  • Intact SNAP + clinical sensory loss = preganglionic injury (pathognomonic)
  • MUAP characteristics indicate nascent reinnervation vs collateral sprouting
  • If no MUAPs at 3 months → proceed with surgical reconstruction

Imaging

  • XR: elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic injury), clavicle/scapula fx, scapulothoracic dissociation
  • CT myelogram: gold standard for pseudomeningoceles (85-95% sensitivity for avulsions)
  • MRI: equivalent sensitivity (~93%), noninvasive, evaluates distal plexus + rotator cuff
  • US: emerging tool for neuroma-in-continuity evaluation

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Suspected neurapraxia/mild axonotmesis with progressive recovery
  • Passive ROM all joints (scapulothoracic to fingers), core/periscapular strengthening
  • Pain management: gabapentin/pregabalin first-line; 75% report significant pain at long-term follow-up; involve pain management early
  • Slings/orthotics to prevent contracture from muscle imbalance

Operative

  • Timing: ideal referral 4-6 weeks; surgery by 3 months if no recovery; do not observe > 6 months

  • Millesi algorithm: stationary Tinel + unchanged MMT + no MUAPs at 3 mo → operate; if Tinel advancing + MUAPs present but no clinical improvement → reassess at 6-week intervals

  • Functional priorities (memorize this order): (1) elbow flexion, (2) shoulder abduction/ER, (3) protective hand sensation, (4) grasp/prehension, (5) elbow extension; intrinsic hand function is nearly impossible to restore in flail limb

Key Reconstruction Strategies

Function Preferred Transfer Alternative
Shoulder abduction/ER SAN→SSN + triceps→axillary (double transfer) C5 graft to PDUT; intercostals
Elbow flexion Double Oberlin (median→brachialis + ulnar→biceps) Intercostals, SAN+graft, FFMT
Elbow extension Intercostals→long head triceps Thoracodorsal, ECRB fascicle
Grasp FFMT (gracilis) Two-stage Doi procedure

Nerve Grafting

  • C5 graft to PDUT for shoulder; C6 graft to ADUT for elbow flexion
  • Sural nerve = workhorse autograft (highest fascicular density; 30-40 cm per leg)
  • Cable graft: lay on tongue depressor, fold back, fibrin glue ends, cut at folds
  • Grafts < 12 cm preferred; > 12 cm = diminishing returns
  • Intraoperative NAP + SSEP to assess root viability

Free Functioning Muscle Transfer (FFMT)

  • Gracilis = workhorse; not constrained by denervation window
  • Single-stage (Giuffre): clavicle anchor → FDP/FPL; intercostals for power, SAN for triceps
  • Two-stage (Doi): stage 1 = elbow flexion + finger extension; stage 2 = finger flexion
  • Vascular: thoracoacromial trunk (deltoid branch best size match)
  • Obturator nerve innervation; tension set at 30° elbow flexion contracture at rest

Complications & Outcomes

  • Intraplexal transfers > nerve grafts for elbow flexion (83% vs 56% M4+)
  • Double transfer for shoulder: 100% ≥ M3, 74% ≥ M4 (mean abduction 122°) vs single: 70% ≥ M3
  • Intercostal nerve harvest: 10% pleural breach rate; respiratory compromise usually transient
  • FFMT flap loss rare but must monitor 5 days (hourly Doppler x 24h)
  • 40% BPI patients do not return to work; ~80% satisfied with quality of life
  • One-third of US patients undergo surgery > 1 year post-injury (suboptimal delays)

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Intact SNAP in area of sensory loss = preganglionic avulsion (cannot graft; must transfer)
  • Always explore panplexus injuries — high likelihood of finding at least one graftable root (usually C5)
  • Do not harvest phrenic + multiple intercostals simultaneously unless carefully weighing respiratory risk
  • Contralateral C7 transfer = controversial; risk of permanent donor-side deficit
  • Even modest reinnervation provides a “helper limb” — set realistic expectations early
  • Psychological support is critical (depression prevalent, financial strain, prolonged recovery)

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A patient with panplexus BPI has absent sensation in the C8-T1 distribution. NCS shows a normal ulnar SNAP. What is the diagnosis? — A: Preganglionic (root avulsion) injury of C8-T1; the DRG is intact so the sensory arc is preserved despite clinical sensory loss.
  2. Q: What is the preferred nerve transfer combination to restore both shoulder abduction and external rotation? — A: SAN-to-suprascapular nerve (ER/supraspinatus) + triceps branch-to-anterior branch of axillary nerve (deltoid abduction); the “double transfer” achieves 100% ≥ M3 in pooled data.
  3. Q: At what time point should surgical reconstruction proceed if serial exams show no recovery from a closed BPI? — A: 3 months if Tinel is stationary and no MUAPs on EMG; do not observe beyond 6 months without intervention.
  4. Q: Why does free functioning muscle transfer (FFMT) remain an option for patients presenting > 18 months after BPI? — A: FFMT brings its own muscle with it (gracilis), so it is not constrained by the 12-18 month window for native muscle denervation atrophy.
