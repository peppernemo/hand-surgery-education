Topic 2 · Nerve II: Injury, Plexus & Tendon Transfers

Nerve injury grading and repair · brachial plexus · nerve and tendon transfers

Traumatic nerve questions are a rising OITE category, and they are almost always management vignettes: what grade, what timing, what operation. The clock (motor endplates) organizes everything.

Interns own: Seddon/Sunderland grading, the 1 mm/day rule, and documenting a nerve exam well enough that recovery can be tracked against it.

Test yourself

  1. Compare Seddon and Sunderland. Which grades recover spontaneously and which need surgery?
  2. A sharp volar forearm laceration transects the median nerve. When and how do you repair it, and how do you follow recovery month to month?
  3. A humeral shaft fracture presents with wrist drop. Observe or explore? What changes if the palsy appears after closed reduction?
  4. Six months after a mid-forearm median repair there is no thenar recovery. Why are motor nerve transfers usually off the table, and what is the workhorse reconstruction?
  5. Name the classic tendon transfer set for a low radial nerve palsy, and the principle behind donor selection.
  6. A motorcyclist has a flail arm, Horner’s syndrome, and an elevated hemidiaphragm. Localize the injury and state why that location changes the surgical options.
  7. Which nerve transfers restore elbow flexion and shoulder function in upper trunk plexus injuries?
Answers
  1. Neurapraxia (I): conduction block, full recovery in weeks. Axonotmesis (II-IV): II regenerates at ~1 mm/day with good recovery; III is variable; IV is a neuroma-in-continuity needing surgery. Neurotmesis (V): always surgical. I-II spontaneous, III variable, IV-V operate.
  2. Sharp transection: primary tension-free epineurial repair within days, under the microscope, fascicles and surface vessels as alignment maps; graft or conduit for any gap under tension. Follow with an advancing Tinel and reinnervation in order (proximal muscles first, thenar last), monthly. Sensory re-education with therapy.
  3. Radial palsy with a closed humeral shaft fracture: observe, most are neurapraxia and recover; splint the wrist, track Tinel. A palsy that appears AFTER manipulation is an indication to explore (entrapment in the fracture site). Open fractures and sharp mechanisms also get explored.
  4. Motor endplates degenerate by 12-18 months, and a transfer still needs months of regeneration; at 6 months with nothing, the math no longer works for motor transfer. Tendon transfer is reliable: EIP opponensplasty for thenar function. Sensory reconstruction has no clock.
  5. PT to ECRB (wrist extension), FCR (or FCU) to EDC (finger extension), PL to rerouted EPL (thumb extension). Principles: expendable donor, adequate excursion and power (donor loses one grade), straight line of pull, synergy, supple joints, one transfer per function.
  6. Horner’s plus hemidiaphragm elevation = preganglionic root avulsion (C8-T1 for Horner’s, C3-5 phrenic involvement proximally). Avulsed roots cannot be grafted; reconstruction must import axons: nerve transfers (spinal accessory, intercostals, contralateral C7) rather than root grafting. Pseudomeningoceles on MRI support avulsion.
  7. Elbow flexion: ulnar-fascicle-to-biceps branch (Oberlin), often doubled with a median fascicle to brachialis. Shoulder: spinal accessory to suprascapular, and a triceps branch to the axillary nerve.
