Wrist Dissociation and Instability (non-SL)

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Proximal row (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform): no tendon attachments — intercalated segment; position dictated by geometry + ligaments
  • Distal row (trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate): rigid platform with negligible intercarpal motion
  • Gilula’s lines: smooth concentric arcs along proximal/distal row joint surfaces on PA; disruption = instability
  • Extrinsic ligaments: volar (RSC, LRL, SRL, ulnocarpal complex) > dorsal (DRC, DIC) in structural strength; dorsal ligaments more proprioceptive
  • Space of Poirier: capsular weakness between RSC and LRL — lunate dislocates volarly through here
  • SLIL: dorsal strongest; LTIL: volar strongest
  • Dart-thrower’s motion (DTM): natural wrist axis (radial extension → ulnar flexion); minimal proximal row motion through this arc

Classification

  • Larsen (1995) — 6 parameters: chronicity, constancy, etiology, location, direction, pattern
  • CID (carpal instability dissociative): within a row (SL, LT)
  • CIND (carpal instability nondissociative): between rows (midcarpal, radiocarpal)
  • CIC (carpal instability complex): perilunate injuries spanning both
  • CIA (carpal instability adaptive): secondary to extracarpal pathology (distal radius malunion)

Carpal Instability Complex (Perilunate Injuries)

  • Mayfield stages (radial → ulnar): I = SL disruption; II = capitate dislocation (space of Poirier opens); III = LT dissociation; IV = lunate dislocation
  • Lesser arc = purely ligamentous; greater arc = fractures through surrounding bones (most commonly transscaphoid)
  • PLIND (perilunate injuries non-dislocated): no capitate-lunate dislocation on lateral; midcarpal osteochondral loose bodies are a clue
  • Up to 25% historically missed; lateral radiograph most diagnostic (“spilled teacup” sign)
  • ORIF: combined volar + dorsal approach. Volar first (CTR + volar capsule [space of Poirier] repair), then dorsal (joystick K-wires to reduce the SL, then 0.062” K-wires for definitive stabilization).
  • Arthroscopic-assisted fixation feasible for PLIND and select PLD (Herzberg stages 1-2A)
  • Post-op: cast 2 months → wrist brace + gentle motion; buried K-wire removal at 3 months
  • Despite severity, 10-15 yr outcomes often satisfactory; delay in treatment = worst prognostic factor

Barrera: Perilunate repair construct — combined volar + dorsal approach. Start volar: CTR (median nerve is almost always under pressure), then repair the volar capsule (space of Poirier). Go dorsal: joystick K-wires into scaphoid + lunate to reduce the SL under direct vision, then 0.062” pins for definitive stabilization. Cast 2 months, transition to wrist brace + gentle motion, buried K-wire removal at 3 months.

Lunotriquetral Interosseous Ligament Injury

  • Less common than SL; often associated with TFCC tears and ulnar impaction
  • Geissler classification applies (same as SL)
  • VISI pattern (lunate flexion) — only with complete LTIL + secondary stabilizer failure
  • Cadaveric: 30% LTIL perforation in patients > 60 yo
  • Exam: LT ballottement (Reagan test), LT shear (Kleinman test), LT compression; ulnar-sided wrist pain
  • Imaging: disrupted Gilula’s arcs I/II, proximal triquetral migration; diastasis rare even with instability
  • Treatment ladder:
    • Conservative: immobilization (pisiform-supported cast), therapy, CSI
    • Arthroscopic debridement + pinning (80% success, Osterman series)
    • Ligament reconstruction (distally based ECU strip through bone tunnels) — 69% 5-yr survival (Shin)
    • LT arthrodesis: 41% nonunion rate — generally avoided
    • Ulnar shortening osteotomy: effective when ulnocarpal abutment coexists (83% good-to-excellent, Mirza)

Barrera: For suspected TFCC injuries with associated LT symptoms, start with the standard ladder: 4 weeks brace → Wrist Widget + OT → MRI without contrast → CSI → surgery as last resort. Most stable-DRUJ injuries respond to immobilization and bracing.

  • The LT component often improves in parallel with TFCC-directed conservative treatment.

Carpal Instability Nondissociative (CIND)

Palmar Midcarpal Instability (PMCI)

  • Most common CIND; often in generalized ligamentous laxity
  • “Catch-up clunk”: proximal row lags in flexion during ulnar deviation → snaps into extension
  • “Kettle sign”: difficulty holding heavy objects in ulnar deviation
  • Radiographs often normal; dynamic fluoroscopy demonstrates clunk
  • Treatment: primarily nonsurgical (6+ months); ECU/FCU strengthening, pisiform-padded orthosis
  • Surgical: dorsal capsular plication (von Schroeder: durable improvement at 35 months); radiolunate fusion preserves DTM; soft tissue reconstruction has high failure rate (6/9 in Lichtman series)

Dorsal Midcarpal Instability (DMCI / CLIP Wrist)

  • Dorsal capitate subluxation on lunate (CIND-DISI)
  • Pain with heavy lifting + power grasp in supination
  • Johnson-Carroll: space of Poirier obliteration (6 excellent/11 outcomes)
  • Treatment: primarily nonsurgical

Combined Midcarpal Instability (CMCI)

  • Young women with laxity; VISI deformity + dorsal subluxation on provocative testing
  • May respond to volar ligamentous reefing (Apergis: 13/14 excellent); but at 5-8 yr follow-up, no difference surgical vs nonsurgical

Radiocarpal Instability / Fracture-Dislocations

  • High-energy; dorsal dislocation more common
  • Dumontier classification: type 1 (< 1/3 scaphoid facet, RSC avulsion) vs type 2 (> 1/3 facet fragment)
  • Type 1: volar ligament repair + dorsal spanning plate protection (3 months before plate removal)
  • Type 2: ORIF of radial styloid fragment restores stability

Carpal Instability Adaptive (CIA)

  • Most commonly from distal radius malunion (dorsal tilt → DISI → midcarpal catch-up clunk)
  • Treatment: corrective osteotomy of distal radius; 3D virtual planning available

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Always get a true lateral radiograph — most perilunate injuries are diagnosed here
  • PLIND lesions: look for midcarpal osteochondral loose bodies as a diagnostic clue
  • LT arthrodesis has unacceptably high nonunion (41%) — reconstruction or ulnar shortening preferred
  • Midcarpal instability is primarily nonsurgical — commit to 6 months of therapy before considering surgery
  • For radiocarpal fracture-dislocations, protect the volar ligament repair for 3 full months
  • Dart-thrower’s motion rehab is relevant across multiple instability patterns

Board-Style Questions (3-5)

  1. Q: In Mayfield’s progressive perilunar instability, what structure fails in stage III? — A: The lunotriquetral interosseous ligament.
  2. Q: A patient with midcarpal instability demonstrates a painful clunk as the wrist moves into ulnar deviation with the proximal row snapping from flexion into extension. What is this sign called? — A: The catch-up clunk (palmar midcarpal instability).
  3. Q: What is the reported nonunion rate for lunotriquetral arthrodesis? — A: 41% (Shin series).
  4. Q: A perilunate fracture-dislocation with a scaphoid waist fracture is classified as what type of arc injury? — A: Greater arc injury.
  5. Q: What surgical option for palmar midcarpal instability best preserves dart-thrower’s motion? — A: Radiolunate fusion.
