Polydactyly

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Most common congenital hand difference: 23.4 per 10,000 live births
  • Ulnar polydactyly in African Americans: up to 1 in 143 (autosomal dominant, variable penetrance)
  • Radial polydactyly in Caucasians/Asians: 1 in 3,000 (usually unilateral, spontaneous)
  • Triphalangeal thumb: higher hereditary predisposition (chromosome 7q36)
  • Central polydactyly: may be linked to HOXD13 mutation (chromosome 2)
  • Ulnar polydactyly in Caucasians may indicate syndrome (Ellis-van Creveld, Bardet-Biedl, Pallister-Hall)

Classification

Radial Polydactyly — Wassel (Flatt)

  • Based on level of bone/joint involvement
  • Type IV most common (29-43%); Type I least common
  • Interobserver reliability kappa = 0.65
  • Rotterdam classification: expanded to include triphalangeal variants; 40% of Wassel-unclassifiable cases captured

Ulnar Polydactyly — Stelling-Turek / Temtany-McKusick

System Types
Stelling-Turek I: skin bridge; II: articulates with bifid MC head; III: entire ray duplicated
Temtany-McKusick A: well-developed, bony; B: rudimentary, pedunculated
  • Type B is 10x more frequent in African descent, bilateral in 70%

Central Polydactyly

  • Buck-Gramcko classification (level of duplication)
  • Wall radiographic classification for synpolydactyly: excellent reliability (kappa 0.87-0.97)

Treatment

General Principles

  • Prefer term “split thumb” over “duplicated thumb” — retained thumb will always be smaller
  • Surgery typically at ~1 year (before pinch development, lower anesthesia risk)
  • Ablate nonfunctional parts but retain any usable component for stability, motion, function
  • Maximize skeletal length and joint stability
  • Keep periosteal sleeve for intrinsic muscles and collateral ligaments
  • Closing wedge osteotomies heal faster than opening wedge

  • 20° angular deformity requires closing wedge osteotomy (soft tissue rebalancing alone insufficient)

Radial Polydactyly Techniques

  • Simple excision: no bony connection, dominant thumb adequate; traction neurectomy to avoid neuroma
  • Excision and reconstruction: preserve collateral from minor thumb via periosteal sleeve; contour widened bone; K-wire protection; tendon realignment for pollex abductus
  • Bilhaut-Cloquet: combine outer portions of two similarly hypoplastic thumbs; modified to preserve one articular surface
  • On-top plasty: proximal of one + distal of other; preserve NV bundle

Ulnar Polydactyly

  • Type B: suture ligation, surgical clip, or formal office excision with local anesthesia + traction neurectomy (high satisfaction)
  • Type A: formal surgical excision and reconstruction as for radial polydactyly

Central Polydactyly

  • Challenging; ray resection with web space and transverse MC ligament reconstruction

  • Corrective osteotomies commonly required

  • Functional and aesthetic results often inferior to radial/ulnar polydactyly

  • Ulnar polydactyly type B in a Black child is most often autosomal dominant and benign — office excision with local is appropriate

  • Ulnar polydactyly in a Caucasian child should prompt syndromic workup (family history + screen for other anomalies) before assuming isolated

Complications & Outcomes

  • Reconstructed thumb will be smaller in length and girth than unaffected thumb even at skeletal maturity
  • 36-year follow-up (Gholson): no difference in pinch/grip strength vs unaffected; 46% angular deformity but no pain; 15% IP joint arthritis
  • 10-year follow-up (Stutz): lateral/tripod/tip pinch 96/86/92% of unaffected; 19% reoperation (mostly IP arthrodesis)
  • Satisfaction decreases significantly with joint angulation
  • Type B ligation may leave small (~2 mm) residual bump

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Wassel Type IV is most common radial polydactyly — know this for boards
  • Ulnar polydactyly in Caucasians = think syndromes; in African Americans = usually isolated AD
  • Angular deformity > 20° needs osteotomy, not just soft tissue work
  • Don’t use extensor mechanism for collateral reconstruction (creates checkrein effect)
  • Central polydactyly has the worst outcomes of all three types

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: What is the most common type of radial polydactyly in the Wassel classification? — A: Type IV (duplication at the level of the proximal phalanx).
  2. Q: A Caucasian infant presents with bilateral ulnar polydactyly. What should be done beyond surgical planning? — A: Evaluate for associated syndromes (Ellis-van Creveld, Bardet-Biedl, Pallister-Hall) — ulnar polydactyly in Caucasians may indicate an underlying syndrome.
  3. Q: After radial polydactyly reconstruction, residual angular deformity of 25° is noted. What is the appropriate intervention? — A: Closing wedge osteotomy — angular deformity > 20° will not correct with soft tissue rebalancing alone.
  4. Q: What is the most common complication at long-term follow-up after radial polydactyly reconstruction? — A: Angular deformity (46% at 36-year follow-up), though most patients are pain-free.
