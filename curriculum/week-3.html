Week 3 · Fractures I: The Wrist

Resident track, week 3 of 7 · Intern track, week 3 of 4 (distal radius portion)

The distal radius is the most common long-bone fracture in adults and the most frequent operative case in a hand practice. The scaphoid is the most commonly missed carpal injury, and a missed scaphoid becomes a nonunion and then a SNAC wrist. This week is about reduction parameters, who needs surgery, and never missing the occult injury.

Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. State the acceptable reduction parameters for a distal radius fracture: radial height, radial inclination, volar tilt, articular step-off.
  2. Why do proximal-pole scaphoid fractures behave differently from distal-pole fractures? Describe the blood supply.
  3. Snuffbox tenderness with a “normal” x-ray 4 days after a fall: what is the sensitivity of the initial film, and what are your two acceptable pathways?
  4. A 55-year-old independent, right-hand-dominant woman has an intra-articular distal radius fracture with 3 mm step-off and 25 degrees dorsal angulation. What do you recommend, and what did the WRIST trial show for patients over 60?
  5. What is a Galeazzi fracture, a Monteggia fracture, and an Essex-Lopresti injury?
  6. List the common complications after distal radius ORIF and the warning sign for each.
  7. Name the Mayfield stages of perilunate instability and the radiographic finding that gives away a perilunate dislocation on the lateral view.
Answer key
  1. Radial height within 2-3 mm of contralateral (~11 mm normal); radial inclination >= 15 degrees (normal ~22); volar tilt from neutral to 10 degrees dorsal acceptable (normal ~11 volar); articular step-off <= 2 mm (<= 1 mm for young, active patients); DRUJ congruent.
  2. The dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery enters at the dorsal ridge and flows retrograde, distal to proximal, so the proximal pole is end-vascular: highest nonunion and AVN risk. Distal pole/tubercle fractures have the best supply and usually heal in a cast.
  3. Initial x-ray sensitivity ~70-80%. Pathways: thumb spica immobilization with repeat films in 10-14 days, or early MRI (near 100% sensitive; CT if MRI unavailable). Go straight to MRI for a laborer or athlete who cannot afford empiric immobilization. A missed scaphoid becomes nonunion, then SNAC.
  4. Step-off > 2 mm with 25 degrees dorsal tilt in an active, independent patient: ORIF with a volar locking plate. WRIST trial: in patients >= 60, nonoperative care had equivalent 1-year patient-rated outcomes to operative fixation, so nonop is defensible for the low-demand elderly patient; her functional independence argues for surgery.
  5. Galeazzi: radial shaft fracture + DRUJ disruption. Monteggia: ulnar shaft fracture + radial head dislocation. Essex-Lopresti: radial head fracture + interosseous membrane disruption + DRUJ injury (longitudinal forearm instability).
  6. EPL rupture: cannot retropulse the thumb (attritional at Lister’s or dorsal screw); treat with EIP-to-EPL transfer. FPL irritation/rupture: plate past the watershed line (Soong grade); pain or crepitus with thumb flexion; remove the plate. Acute median neuropathy: progressive numbness after injury or reduction; emergent carpal tunnel release. Stiffness: start finger motion day 1. CRPS: vigilance in the first 6 weeks.
  7. Mayfield I-IV: scapholunate, capitolunate, lunotriquetral, then lunate dislocation (“spilled teapot” volar). On the lateral, the capitate no longer sits in the lunate cup; on the PA, Gilula’s arcs are broken. Median nerve symptoms are common; this is a same-day reduction.

Run the check-in

Cases to walk through:

  1. ★ A 62-year-old woman falls on an outstretched hand at home. Dorsally angulated intra-articular distal radius fracture. Take it from ED reduction (your technique, your block) through the surgery-vs-cast conversation, and defend your recommendation with the trial data.
  2. A 24-year-old fell playing basketball, has snuffbox tenderness, and films are negative. Walk through your plan, including exactly what you write in the discharge instructions and why you will not be talked out of immobilizing him.
  3. A 30-year-old high-energy fall has a “sprained wrist” per the outside read. You notice the capitate is not seated in the lunate on the lateral. What just happened, what do you do tonight, and what is the definitive plan?
