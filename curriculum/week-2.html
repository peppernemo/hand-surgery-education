Week 2 · Infections & Soft-Tissue Emergencies

Resident track, week 2 of 7 · Intern track, week 2 of 4

This is the week that covers most overnight hand consults: flexor tenosynovitis (FTS), fight bite, compartment syndrome, high-pressure injection, necrotizing fasciitis, and the fingertip. These diagnoses are made at the bedside, and delay costs function. Know the exam findings cold and know which ones change the timeline.

Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. Name the four Kanavel signs. Which appears first and which is most specific?
  2. A patient punched someone in the mouth 18 hours ago and has a 3 mm laceration over the middle-finger MCP with pain on active flexion. Diagnosis, workup, and management?
  3. A painter has a pinhole entry wound at the index pulp from a paint gun 4 hours ago. The finger looks pink and pain is mild. What do you do and why?
  4. At what compartment pressure (or delta pressure) do you take a forearm to the OR, and what wins when the numbers and the exam disagree?
  5. Differentiate felon, paronychia, and herpetic whitlow: location, etiology, and the one you must NOT incise.
  6. Rapidly spreading erythema, dusky skin, crepitus, gas on x-ray, LRINEC 9. Next step, and what is the role of the ED vs OR here?
  7. State the absolute and relative indications for replantation, and the ischemia windows for a digit vs a muscle-containing part.
Answer key
  1. Kanavel: fusiform swelling, flexed resting posture, tenderness along the flexor sheath, pain with passive extension. Fusiform swelling and sheath tenderness tend to appear early; pain with passive extension is the most specific. FTS is a surgical urgency: IV antibiotics, hand elevation, and OR sheath irrigation for the established case; a very early presentation (< 24 h) can occasionally be trialed on IV antibiotics with serial exams by the surgical team.
  2. Fight bite. OR for formal I&D regardless of how benign the wound looks: the tooth violates the extensor hood and often the MCP joint with the fist clenched, then the tracts seal at rest. X-ray for tooth fragment, fracture, air. Ampicillin-sulbactam empirically (covers Eikenella). Arthrotomy, irrigation, leave the skin open or loosely approximated, IV antibiotics.
  3. High-pressure injection is an operative emergency despite the benign look. Debridement delay beyond ~6 hours sharply raises amputation rates (paint and diesel worst). Extensile exposure, serial debridements, IV antibiotics, counsel about amputation up front.
  4. Delta pressure (diastolic minus compartment) < 30 mmHg, or absolute pressure > 30-45 mmHg. The exam wins: pain out of proportion plus pain with passive stretch is enough to go to the OR without a needle. Volar forearm fasciotomy at JHMC is ulnar-based (preserves the radial forearm flap donor site), plus dorsal, mobile wad, carpal tunnel, and hand releases as indicated.
  5. Felon: pulp abscess in the fibrous septa; I&D through a lateral or distal volar midline incision, never a fishmouth. Paronychia: nail-fold infection; lift the fold or remove part of the nail; early cases can be soaked. Herpetic whitlow: HSV, vesicles on an erythematous base; do NOT incise (superinfection, prolonged shedding); acyclovir and dry dressing.
  6. Necrotizing fasciitis: straight to the OR. Broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin for toxin suppression), aggressive resuscitation, wide debridement without waiting for imaging. Serial debridements every 24-48 h. The diagnosis is clinical and surgical.
  7. Absolute: thumb at any level, multiple digits, transmetacarpal/partial hand, any pediatric amputation, wrist or forearm with a sharp mechanism. Relative: single digit distal to the FDS insertion. Relative contraindications: single digit in zone II, severe crush/avulsion, prolonged warm ischemia. Windows: digit ~12 h warm / 24 h cold; muscle-containing parts ~6 h warm / 12 h cold.

Run the check-in

Cases to walk through:

  1. ★ A 45-year-old diabetic presents 3 days after a rose-thorn puncture with a swollen index finger held in flexion, sheath tenderness, and pain with passive extension. Take it from ED arrival to the OR: antibiotics, admission orders, consent, and what you set up in the room.
  2. A 32-year-old punched a tooth 18 hours ago; 3 mm dorsal MCP wound, pain with flexion. Door to OR: workup, antibiotic choice, what you find at arthrotomy, and what you tell him about stiffness.
  3. A 25-year-old has a crushed forearm, pain out of proportion, and worsening pain with passive finger extension 6 hours post-injury. Walk through your decision to measure or not measure pressures, the fasciotomy incisions you would draw, and the post-op plan.
