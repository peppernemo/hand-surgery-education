AVN of Hand and Wrist

Anatomy / Basic Science

Lunate (Kienböck Disease)

  • “Keystone” of wrist — load-bearing intercalated segment; 5 articulations, no muscle attachments
  • Viegas classification: Type I (35%, no hamate facet) — more prevalent in Kienböck, coronal fracture-prone; Type II (65%, hamate facet present) — may be protective
  • Zapico classification: Type 1 trapezoidal (negative ulnar variance, nutcracker susceptibility); Type 2 rectangular (neutral); Type 3 pentagonal (positive)
  • Proximal subchondral bone plate only 0.1 mm thick — first and most severely affected region
  • Blood supply: mainly palmar (radiocarpal + intercarpal arches); single arterial supply → increased AVN risk (Gelberman 1980)
  • Pathogenesis (“compartment syndrome of bone”): repetitive loading → venous occlusion → interstitial edema → sinusoidal tamponade → ischemia → necrosis → repair/fracture/fragmentation

Other AVN Sites

  • Scaphoid (Preiser): retrograde blood supply → proximal pole vulnerability; dominant hand, F > M
  • Capitate: retrograde supply like scaphoid; do not osteotomize proximal to waist (risk to subarticular plexus)
  • MC head (Dietrich): 3rd MC most common; 35% of cadavers lack central arteriole; M:F 3:2, peak 3rd decade

Classification

Kienböck — Lichtman-Bain Algorithm

Combines osseous staging + arthroscopic assessment + Schmitt vascular (ceMRI): - B1 (intact cortex + cartilage): protect/unload - B2 (localized disease): reconstruct - B3 (advanced lunate disease): salvage - C1 (carpal instability, intact articulations): stabilize - C2a (radiolunate compromised): bypass or fuse - C2b (radioscaphoid compromised): PRC if lunate facet OK - C3 (KDAC — Kienböck disease advanced collapse): salvage

Preiser — Herbert-Lanzetta Staging

  1. Normal x-ray, abnormal CT
  2. Increased density proximal pole + osteopenia
  3. Fragmentation ± pathologic fracture
  4. Fragmentation + collapse + OA

Capitate — Milliez Classification

  • Group I (proximal), Group II (body), Group III (whole bone)

Workup

History

  • Kienböck: dorsal wrist pain worse with extension; age 20-40, M:F 2:1; average 1-2 years of symptoms before diagnosis
  • Pediatric Kienböck: often regresses with conservative care (< 12 yo)

Imaging

  • Plain radiographs: 3 views; assess Lichtman stage, ulnar variance, carpal collapse
  • CT: fragmentation, coronal fracture, perilunate arthritis
  • Gadolinium-enhanced MRI: Schmitt viability patterns — normal enhancement vs ischemic (maintained perfusion) vs partial necrosis (reparative) vs necrotic (no enhancement)
  • 4D-CT: dynamic kinematics; SPECT-CT: bone scan sensitivity + CT anatomy

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Pediatric (< 15 yo): nonoperative first — most resolve with splinting
  • Ages 16-20: nonoperative trial then consider unloading
  • Elderly (> 70 yo): nonoperative; consider synovectomy
  • Splinting 4-12 weeks (recent: 12 wk cast for < 17 yo without collapse); NSAIDs, PT

Operative (by Lunate/Wrist State)

Unloading (B1 disease): - Radial shortening osteotomy: for ulnar-negative; 2 mm resection optimal (> 4 mm = poor outcome); volar locking plate - Capitate shortening: for ulnar-neutral/positive; single cut at middle/distal third; headless screw - Camembert osteotomy (wedge from ulnar radius) = most complete unloading per FEA - Core decompression of radius (Illaramendi): 22 pts, no further surgery at 10 yr

Reconstruction (B2/B3): - PRC: for less active, older patients; grip 68% contralateral; capitate-radius arthritis in 87% (often asymptomatic) - Radioscapholunate fusion: excise distal scaphoid pole (improves motion + union), excise triquetrum for graft - Scaphocapitate fusion: bypasses lunate; requires functional scaphoid fossa - VBG (vascularized bone graft): 4+5 ECA or medial femoral trochlea; for B1-B2 with ulnar-neutral/positive

Salvage (C3): - Total wrist fusion (10-15° extension + slight ulnar deviation) - Wrist hemiarthroplasty + PRC

  • For suspected Kienböck, gadolinium-enhanced MRI (Schmitt classification) directly informs whether the lunate has viable perfusion and changes the treatment algorithm.

Preiser Disease

  • Conservative: immobilization, steroid, electrical stimulation
  • Advanced collapse → 3-corner fusion (preferred — excise triquetrum for graft + better ulnar deviation)

Complications & Outcomes

  • All Kienböck evidence is level IV / small retrospective — no quality RCT exists
  • Bain series (31 pts, 10-yr f/u): 23% required further surgery (mostly hardware removal); no salvage procedures, infections, or nonunions

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • At-risk lunate: Viegas type I + Zapico type 1 + negative ulnar variance + flat radial inclination
  • Kienböck is NOT synonymous with ulnar-negative variance — later studies show inconsistent association
  • Capitate AVN: never osteotomize proximal to the waist (retrograde blood supply)
  • Pyrocarbon lunate replacement is not routinely recommended (instability concern)
  • Dietrich disease of the MC head may coexist with Freiberg disease (metatarsal head AVN)

Board-Style Questions (3-5)

  1. Q: A 28-year-old manual laborer with dorsal wrist pain has ulnar-negative variance and a sclerotic lunate with intact cortex on x-ray. Gadolinium MRI shows homogeneous proximal enhancement. What is the recommended initial surgical treatment? — A: Radial shortening osteotomy (B1 disease, ulnar-negative, viable lunate).
  2. Q: What Viegas lunate type is more prevalent in Kienböck disease? — A: Type I (no hamate facet).
  3. Q: In Preiser disease with advanced scaphoid collapse and OA, what is the preferred salvage procedure? — A: Three-corner fusion with triquetral excision.
  4. Q: What percentage of cadaveric metacarpal heads lack a central arteriole, predisposing to Dietrich disease? — A: 35%.
  5. Q: What is the maximum recommended resection for radial shortening osteotomy in Kienböck disease? — A: 2 mm (> 4 mm associated with poor outcomes).
