Fingertip Injuries

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Pulp: glabrous skin with dense fibrous septae compartmentalizing fat over distal phalanx (P3)
  • Nail unit: germinal matrix (proximal, produces nail), sterile matrix (distal, adherence), eponychium, hyponychium
  • Vascular supply: proper digital arteries → terminal branches at P3 tuft
  • Sensory: proper digital nerves → terminal branches; no large neuromas form at fingertip (distal to PDN branching)
  • P3 tuft fracture nonunion rate: 55% (Da Cruz)

Workup

History

  • Mechanism: crush (most common) vs sharp vs avulsion (reflexive withdrawal = longitudinal NV injury)
  • Smoking/nicotine, DM, vascular disease — predict marginal necrosis
  • Contamination + delay → consider staged treatment

Exam

  • Map all wounds (glabrous + matrix); trace perfusion pathways to each flap
  • Predict long-term viability

Imaging

  • Plain radiographs: soft tissue thickness over bone, fracture characterization
  • Acoustic Doppler: evaluate arterial network for flap planning

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Pure crush, no fracture, no open wound: reassurance, pain control, daily active DIP motion, pinch as tolerated; no splint needed
  • Subungual hematoma: no percentage threshold demands nail removal if dorsal cortex intact + nail plate not avulsed
  • Sharp laceration, clean edges, no bone exposure, <15 mm: non-surgical reasonable; average healing 27 days
  • Semi-occlusive moisturizing dressings changed weekly

Barrera: Secondary healing with a moist dressing — Vaseline (or bacitracin) under Coban, changed daily — is my default for fingertip injuries and partial amputations, unless there’s a specific operative indication. Patients avoid unnecessary surgery and do better than you’d expect. Hand them Lalonde’s PRS-GO 2022 article (it has the words and the videos) and tell them exactly when to quit: red is raw, pink is healed.

Lalonde Secondary-Healing Protocol

Lalonde DH, Bouhtiauy J. Secondary healing of fingertip amputations: simple wound care advice for patients. PRS-GO 2022;10:e4020. Open-access full text with patient videos: https://journals.lww.com/prsgo/fulltext/2022/01000/secondary_healing_of_fingertip_amputations__simple.26.aspx

Principle: raw tissue kept moist will heal. Skin, fat, nerve, and blood vessels are pulled over the exposed bone/tendon by wound contracture as the defect shrinks closed over 4–8 weeks — no surgery required.

Daily instructions for the patient:

  • Shower with a naked finger — don’t aim the nozzle at the tip (it hurts); just let water, with or without soap, run over the wound.
  • Apply thick Vaseline (or bacitracin) over all the raw tissue — too much is better than too little (too little and the bandage sticks and hurts coming off).
  • Wrap with Coban — a clean, not sterile, self-adherent bandage that holds the Vaseline on so the wound can’t dry.
  • Repeat daily after the shower, which rinses away the old Vaseline and debris.
  • Don’t pick at it. At about a week there may be an odor on unwrapping — gently lift off the old Vaseline with a Q-tip; don’t scrub.
  • Stop when it’s all pink — red is raw, pink is healed. Each day pink healed skin closes over the shrinking red area; stop the Vaseline and Coban once no red remains. The wrinkled, macerated look of the surrounding pink skin is normal and resolves once dressings stop.

Operative

Soft tissue repair: - Debride devitalized tissue; fit remaining pieces like a puzzle - Small openings (<10 mm wide, 5 mm deep) heal by secondary intention - Matrix repair: identify oblique cleavage planes; foil stent from suture packet in dorsal nail fold to prevent synechiae (split nail) - Sutures: 5-0/6-0 chromic (glabrous), 6-0/7-0 (matrix)

Tuft (P3) management: - Options: allow natural position, reduce + pin (0.028” K-wires), or excise - Keep dorsal cortex level to support matrix + nail plate - Distal small fragments embed in scar, typically asymptomatic by ~2 months

Skin grafts: - FTSG only at fingertip (STSG cannot withstand shear) - Donor: ulnar palm (up to 2.5 x 8 cm) - Match exact defect dimensions; perforate for drainage

Eponymous flaps: - Atasoy V-Y: small transverse/dorsal-oblique defects; ~1 cm advancement; risk of volar contracture + hook nail

  • Kutler dual V-Y: slightly larger/more volar defects; narrow bases = survival risk
  • Moberg (volar thumb): IP flexion contracture risk; custom homodigital PDA flap often yields better result

Pedicle flaps: - CAP (Central Artery of Pulp) flap: small distal defects with exposed bone; replaces Atasoy for better mobility + no hook nail - Antegrade PDA flap (workhorse): moderate volar/larger defects; combined neurovascular pedicle (innervated by PDN); PIP flexion contracture up to 15° in 50%; FTSG to ulnar palm for donor site

  • Retrograde PDA flap: larger defects + DIP crease involvement; PDN dissected away and left in situ; best with 5-7 day physiologic delay; always have backup plan

Free flaps: - SPBRA flap: highly customizable (1-10 cm), directly innervated by palmar cutaneous branch of median nerve, patient satisfaction generally greater than toe donor - Toe donor: bone + matrix + skin composite possible; foot donor heals slowly — many patients refuse

Complications & Outcomes

  • Nail deformity: up to 39% with P3 fracture; hook nail from sterile matrix/tuft mismatch
  • Tuft nonunion: 55% of tuft fractures; often asymptomatic; allow 3-4 months before revision
  • Infection: 1-6% baseline; >24h delay with heavy contamination increases risk
  • Hyperesthesia 19-27%, hypoesthesia 24-40%, cold sensitivity 25-73% — time + desensitization (no procedural intervention)
  • Sensibility: directly innervated flaps static 2PD 3.5-4.7 mm; reinnervated junctures 6.3-11 mm
  • TAM: pedicle flaps average 235°

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Foil stent in dorsal nail fold prevents split nail synechiae — easy step, big impact
  • FTSG only at fingertip; STSG will not survive shear forces
  • Antegrade PDA flap is the workhorse for moderate fingertip defects
  • Retrograde PDA benefits from 5-7 day physiologic delay to minimize flap ischemia
  • 55% tuft fracture nonunion is expected and usually asymptomatic
  • Always discuss secondary healing vs graft vs flap — patient preferences vary widely
  • Subungual hematoma percentage is NOT an indication for nail removal if cortex intact

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: What is the nonunion rate for tuft fractures of P3? — A: 55%; most are asymptomatic and do not require revision.

  2. Q: A patient has a 2 cm volar oblique fingertip amputation of the long finger with exposed bone. What is the workhorse flap? — A: Antegrade homodigital proper digital artery (PDA) flap.

  3. Q: What type of skin graft is appropriate for fingertip coverage? — A: Full-thickness skin graft only; STSG cannot withstand fingertip shear forces. Donor site: ulnar palm.

  4. Q: After nail bed repair, what prevents split nail deformity from synechiae between germinal matrix and dorsal nail fold? — A: Foil stent (from suture packet) placed beneath the dorsal nail fold.
