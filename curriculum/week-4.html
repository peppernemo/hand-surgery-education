Week 4 · Fractures II: The Hand

Resident track, week 4 of 7 · Intern track, week 3 of 4 (metacarpal portion)

Metacarpal and phalangeal fractures fill the cast room, and the decisions are pattern-based: location, angulation, rotation. Rotation is never acceptable; angulation tolerance depends on the digit. The thumb base and the PIP joint are where the traps live.

Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. State the acceptable apex-dorsal angulation for a metacarpal neck fracture of each finger, index through small, and the finding that is never acceptable.
  2. What is the difference between a Bennett, a Rolando, and a reverse Bennett fracture? Name the deforming forces on a Bennett.
  3. How does percentage of articular involvement guide treatment of a dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation?
  4. A 35-year-old has a dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation with 45% volar articular involvement. What are the three surgical options and which is the modern choice?
  5. How do you examine a thumb UCL injury, what laxity is diagnostic, and what is a Stener lesion?
  6. How do you clinically check rotational alignment of a finger fracture?
  7. Board-style: a 19-year-old punches a wall, closed small-finger metacarpal neck fracture, 45 degrees angulated, no rotational deformity. Operate or not? Same fracture in the index?
Answer key
  1. Roughly 10-15 degrees index, 15-20 middle, 30-40 ring, 40-50 small (increasing CMC mobility compensates ulnarly). Any rotational deformity is a surgical indication. Dr. Barrera’s practice for the classic boxer’s fracture without rotation: angulation up to ~70 degrees is treated with buddy taping and early motion (Martinez-Catalan RCT showed equivalent outcomes to reduction and casting).
  2. Bennett: two-part intra-articular thumb metacarpal base fracture; the volar-ulnar beak fragment stays reduced (anterior oblique ligament) while APL pulls the shaft proximally and radially and adductor pollicis adducts and supinates it. Rolando: comminuted Y/T intra-articular pattern, worse prognosis. Reverse Bennett: fifth metacarpal base, shaft pulled by ECU.
  3. < 30-40%: usually stable after reduction; extension-block splint and early motion. 30-50%: tenuous; often operative. > 50%: unstable; needs articular reconstruction. Stability through a full arc of flexion on lateral fluoro is the real test.
  4. Hemi-hamate autograft (modern choice for an acute 30-60% single-fragment injury), volar plate arthroplasty (legacy Eaton option), and dynamic external fixation (best for comminuted pilon patterns).
  5. Stress the MCP in extension (proper UCL) and at 30 degrees flexion (accessory UCL). More than 30 degrees of laxity or 15 degrees side-to-side difference means complete tear. Stener lesion: the adductor aponeurosis interposes between the torn ligament and its insertion; it cannot heal and needs open repair.
  6. Flex the fingers toward the palm: all nails should point toward the scaphoid tubercle with no scissoring; compare cascade to the other hand. Check with the fingers flexed, not extended.
  7. Small finger at 45 degrees without rotation: nonoperative (buddy tape, early motion). Index at 45 degrees: operate; the index CMC is rigid and cannot compensate.

Run the check-in

Cases to walk through:

  1. ★ A 19-year-old punches a wall: small-finger metacarpal neck fracture, 50 degrees angulated. The ED wants to reduce and cast. Talk through your evaluation (especially rotation), your actual treatment, and the conversation with the patient about the bump on his hand.
  2. A 20-year-old has a thumb metacarpal base fracture with a small retained volar-ulnar fragment and a proximally displaced shaft. Name it, draw the deforming forces, and take it to the OR: reduction maneuver, fixation choice.
  3. A 35-year-old caught a ball wrong 3 weeks ago; the ring-finger PIP is swollen and she cannot fully extend it. Films show a dorsal fracture-dislocation with 45% volar lip involvement, still subluxated. Options, your pick, and why the 3-week delay matters.
