Soft Tissue Tumors of the Hand and Upper Extremity

Workup

History + Exam

  • Painless mass, often long-standing; recent change in size or pain = red flag
  • Large + deep + increasing = best malignancy predictor (Nandra); pain alone not useful
  • Refer all superficial > 5 cm or any deep-seated mass to subspecialist
  • Risk increases with age; dorsal wrist sarcoma may mimic ganglion

Imaging

  • Plain XR always (calcifications, osteophytes, pressure erosion)
  • MRI = best for STS — T1 hypo, T2 hyper; Gd guides biopsy to vascular non-necrotic area
  • Specific MRI diagnosis possible only 24% of the time (Kransdorf)
  • F-18 FDG-PET: metastatic detection, benign vs malignant nerve sheath, neoadjuvant response assessment

Biopsy

  • Poor biopsy → amputation; Mankin: 17.3% complication rate, 20% altered subsequent treatment
  • Tourniquet without exsanguination; longitudinal incision incorporable into definitive resection
  • FNA: high false-negative, cannot evaluate architecture — useful for recurrence only
  • Core needle preferred to FNA; non-diagnostic → repeat or open
  • Excisional biopsy for small superficial finger/hand lesions — must excise to negative margins

Barrera: For soft tissue tumor excision in the hand, skip Esmarch exsanguination — elevate only.

Tumor-Like Lesions

Ganglia — Most Common Hand/Wrist Soft Tissue Tumor

  • 50% of all hand masses; 2nd-4th decade

  • Dorsal wrist 70% (arise from dorsal SL ligament), volar wrist 30%
  • 50-60% dorsal wrist ganglia resolve spontaneously
  • Aspiration: 11-85% recurrence (steroids don’t help)
  • Open excision (dissect stalk + adjacent capsule); arthroscopic: 11% recurrence
  • Female + preop pain → most likely persistent postop pain

Epidermal Inclusion Cyst

  • Traumatic implantation of epithelial cells; fingertip in laborers
  • Intraosseous distal phalanx variant may mimic malignancy/infection
  • Marginal excision; ~10% recurrence

Benign Tumors

Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor (GCTTS)

  • 2nd most common hand soft tissue tumor; volar digits most common
  • Painless firm slow-growing nodule fixed to deep structures; may erode bone
  • Al Qattan: Type I (pseudocapsule) vs Type II (non-encapsulated, satellite nodules → higher recurrence)
  • Marginal excision; recurrence 4-44%; ↑ with bone invasion, DIP involvement, tendon/capsule involvement

Glomus Tumor

  • Nailbed/periungual/pulp; ring finger most common; middle-aged women
  • Classic triad: cold intolerance + pain + focal tenderness
  • Love’s pin test + Hildreth’s sign (decreased pain on elevation + tourniquet)
  • US or MRI confirms; marginal excision

Schwannoma (Neurilemoma)

  • Most frequent benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor; ulnar nerve most common in UE
  • Antoni A (cellular) + B (myxoid) areas; S-100 positive; true capsule
  • Mobile transverse, not longitudinal; multiple = NF-2
  • MRI target sign on T2 (specificity 100%, sensitivity 59%)
  • Shell out with microscope; postop dysfunction 2.5-30%

Neurofibroma

  • Within fascicles → harder to excise than schwannoma
  • 90% sporadic; ~10% NF-1 (cafe au lait, axillary freckling, Lisch nodules)
  • Plexiform nearly exclusive to NF-1; can undergo malignant transformation
  • PET-CT distinguishes MPNST from neurofibroma

Lipoma

  • ~50% of all soft tissue tumors; deep UE = 22%
  • May cause nerve compression (CTS from carpal tunnel lipoma, PIN entrapment from parosteal radial lipoma)
  • MRI: isointense to SC fat; marginal excision; ~5% recurrence

Other Benign

  • Nerve lipomatosis (fibrolipomatous hamartoma): median nerve 85%; simple decompression only; macrodactyly in 1/3
  • Fibroma of tendon sheath: low MRI signal all sequences; common recurrence in fingers
  • Infantile digital fibromas: pathognomonic intracellular inclusion bodies; 80% in 1st year; may spontaneously regress

Malignant — Soft Tissue Sarcomas

Overview

  • Epithelioid sarcoma = most common hand STS (23%); synovial sarcoma 15%
  • Most common UE STS overall: undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma > liposarcoma > myxofibrosarcoma > synovial
  • Hematogenous → lung; regional lymph nodes 2-5% (except rhabdo, epithelioid, synovial, clear cell, angiosarcoma)
  • Rosenberg 1982: resection + RT = amputation for survival (70% vs 71%); ↑ local recurrence with limb salvage (19% vs 6%)

Treatment Principles

  • Ray amputation = workhorse for hand STS; Pradhan: 6% local recurrence (ray amp) vs 42% (limb salvage)
  • Preop RT: thicker pseudocapsule, easier NV dissection; lower dose/field/complications than postop
  • Unplanned excisional biopsy: 83% have residual disease — re-excision not associated with adverse outcome (Houdek)

Key Subtypes

  • Epithelioid sarcoma: slow-growing nodule/ulcer mistaken for infection; hand 75% 5-yr DFS; > 5 cm = 25% survival
  • Synovial sarcoma: t(X;18); children/adolescents; chemo-sensitive; 30% peripheral calcification; hand 68% 5-yr DFS
  • Clear cell sarcoma: “malignant melanoma of soft parts”; t(12;22) EWS/ATF1; 20% regional lymph node mets
  • AMFS: distal extremities; high local recurrence due to infiltrative margins; RT improves control
  • MPNST: ~50% from NF-1; NF-1 lifetime risk 8-13%; wide excision; 5-yr survival as low as 40%

Cutaneous Malignancies

  • SCC: 2nd most common cancer worldwide; 1.9-5.2% lymphatic spread; wide excision or Mohs
  • BCC: excellent prognosis; raised papule + central ulceration ± telangiectasias; UV = greatest risk
  • Melanoma: most aggressive cutaneous; acral lentiginous = 29-61% of hand melanomas; subungual variant; BRAF testing; sentinel lymph node biopsy; wide excision (1-2 cm margins)

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Ganglion = most common hand mass; ganglia resolve spontaneously 50-60% of the time
  • GCTTS is the 2nd most common hand soft tissue tumor — not a ganglion; check for bone erosion
  • Glomus tumor triad: cold intolerance, pain, focal tenderness — Love’s pin test + Hildreth’s sign
  • Schwannoma: shell out with microscope; target sign on MRI is 100% specific
  • Epithelioid sarcoma = most common hand STS — easily mistaken for infection or ganglion
  • Poor biopsy at a referring institution may force amputation — biopsy at treating center when possible
  • Acral lentiginous melanoma: disproportionately common in hand — always biopsy pigmented subungual lesions

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A 40-year-old presents with a firm, painless, slow-growing nodule on the volar aspect of the index finger that is fixed to deeper structures. X-ray shows cortical scalloping of the proximal phalanx. What is the most likely diagnosis? — A: Tenosynovial giant cell tumor (GCTTS); the bone erosion, volar digit location, and fixation to deep structures are classic.

  2. Q: A patient with NF-1 presents with a rapidly enlarging, painful mass along the ulnar nerve in the forearm. What diagnosis must be ruled out, and what imaging modality helps differentiate? — A: Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST); F-18 FDG-PET distinguishes MPNST from benign neurofibroma.

  3. Q: What is the most common soft tissue sarcoma of the hand, and what benign condition is it frequently misdiagnosed as? — A: Epithelioid sarcoma; frequently mistaken for infection or inflammatory process due to its slow-growing nodular/ulcerative presentation.

  4. Q: A 55-year-old woman has a painful subungual lesion of the ring finger with cold intolerance. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what two exam findings support it? — A: Glomus tumor; Love’s pin test (point tenderness) and Hildreth’s sign (decreased pain with elevation + tourniquet inflation).

  5. Q: The landmark Rosenberg 1982 trial demonstrated what equivalence between limb-sparing surgery + radiation and amputation for soft tissue sarcomas? — A: Equivalent overall survival (70% vs 71%); limb salvage had higher local recurrence (19% vs 6%) but preserved function.
