Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. Grade trigger finger (Green). What is first-line treatment, how effective is it, and when do you operate?
  2. Describe Dr. Barrera’s corticosteroid injection formulation and technique for a flexor sheath injection.
  3. How do you differentiate De Quervain’s tenosynovitis from thumb CMC arthritis on history and exam?
  4. Name the Dupuytren cords and identify the one that endangers the neurovascular bundle. What makes a patient operative?
  5. Compare collagenase, needle aponeurotomy, and limited fasciectomy for a combined MCP + PIP contracture.
  6. Stage thumb CMC arthritis (Eaton) and give the treatment ladder for stage III.
  7. Dorsal wrist ganglion: origin, exam finding, and the recurrence rates for aspiration vs excision.
Answer key
  1. Green I pre-triggering pain, II active triggering, III locking requiring passive unlocking, IV fixed contracture. First-line: corticosteroid injection into the sheath (~80% success first injection, ~50% second). Up to two injections, then A1 pulley release; go to surgery sooner for grade IV and consider a lower injection threshold in diabetics.
  2. 0.75 mL 1% lidocaine + 0.25 mL Kenalog 40 mg/mL (10 mg triamcinolone), 1 mL total, for every injection site; never Depo-Medrol. Trigger finger: into the sheath at A1; if you feel doughy resistance you are in the tendon, back out slightly until it flows.
  3. De Quervain’s: dorsoradial pain at the first dorsal compartment over the styloid, positive Finkelstein/Eichhoff, younger patient, classically postpartum. CMC arthritis: pain at the thumb base with pinch, positive grind, older patient, x-ray changes. The tenderness is a centimeter apart; the exam separates them.
  4. Pretendinous, central, spiral, lateral, retrovascular. The spiral cord pulls the neurovascular bundle central, volar, and proximal, which is why nerves are at risk at the PIP. Operative indications: MCP contracture ~30 degrees or more, any significant PIP contracture, positive tabletop test with functional complaint.
  5. Collagenase: office-based, good for MCP cords, PIP results and durability worse, recurrence ~47% at 5 years. Needle aponeurotomy: fastest recovery, highest recurrence. Limited fasciectomy: best for PIP disease and recurrence (~20-30% at 5 years) at the cost of a real operation. Combined MCP + PIP disease favors fasciectomy.
  6. Eaton I widening/synovitis, II narrowing with debris < 2 mm, III joint obliterated with larger osteophytes, STT spared, IV pantrapezial. Stage III ladder: CMC orthosis, activity modification, NSAIDs, injection; when function fails despite that, trapeziectomy with or without LRTI or suture-button suspensionplasty.
  7. Arises from the scapholunate interval through a one-way valve stalk; transilluminates; often more symptomatic when small and occult. Aspiration recurs ~50%+; open or arthroscopic excision including the stalk recurs ~5-15%. Volar ganglions sit on the radial artery: Allen’s test first, aspirate with respect or not at all.

Run the check-in (wrap-up format)

Final session is half cases, half debrief.

Cases to walk through:

  1. A 50-year-old has a middle finger that locks in flexion every morning and needs the other hand to unlock it. Grade it, treat it in clinic today (talk through your actual injection), and tell her what happens if it comes back.
  2. A 65-year-old man has a palmar cord to the ring finger: MCP 45 degrees, PIP 30 degrees, tabletop positive. Walk through the three options like you are consenting him, and land on a recommendation.
  3. A 62-year-old woman has two years of thumb base pain, positive grind, Eaton III films. Take her from first visit through the decision for surgery, including what she should expect from a trapeziectomy.

Debrief prompts (come with answers):

  • The best case you saw this rotation, and the one decision in it you now understand differently.
  • One consult where you were unsure and want the replay.
  • Which week’s material you will actually reread before your next in-training exam.
