Finger and Thumb Anomalies

Classification

Constriction Band Syndrome (CBS)

  • 1:2,000 to 1:15,000; no hereditary pattern; amnion adhesion theory
  • Patterson: I = simple ring; II = distal atrophy/lymphedema; III = acrosyndactyly (IIIa-c by web depth); IV = intrauterine amputation

Macrodactyly — Flatt Classification

Type Pathology
I Lipofibromatosis (most common, “true” macrodactyly)
II Neurofibromatosis
III Hyperostosis
IV Hemihypertrophy
  • PIK3CA mosaicism / PI3K-AKT pathway implicated
  • Radial side of hand most commonly affected

Clinodactyly

  • Radioulnar (coronal) plane angulation, most common in small finger (ulnar deviation)
  • Delta phalanx (longitudinal epiphyseal bracket) limits growth on affected side → progressive angulation
  • Clinically relevant: > 10°; present in > 1% of normal children

Symphalangism — Flatt / Baek Classifications

  • Absence of IP joint motion (PIP most common); NOG gene mutation
  • Baek: fibrous (10-20° passive motion) → cartilaginous (minimal) → bony (none)

Camptodactyly Types

  • Type I (Infantile): birth, M = F, very responsive to splinting
  • Type II (Adolescent): preadolescent growth spurt, F > M, less responsive
  • Type III (Syndromic): infants with other conditions, often severe

Congenital Clasped Thumb — Tsuyuguchi

  • Type I: passively extendable; Type II: not passively extendable; Type III: arthrogryposis-associated
  • Due to hypoplasia/absence of EPB and/or EPL

Workup

Exam

  • Bunnell test (camptodactyly): PIP flexion less with MCP extended = intrinsic tightness
  • Notta’s nodule (trigger thumb): palpable at MCP volar crease; thumb locked in IP flexion

Treatment

CBS

  • Simple rings: excision with z-plasty or modified w-plasty; Upton technique (excise all scar to fascia, mobilize fat)
  • Acrosyndactyly: early release by 6 months for mild cases
  • Thumb + 2 digits allows chuck pinch, precision/power pinch, hook grip, power grasp

Macrodactyly

  • Amputation favored: isolated digital, progressive, very stiff/angulated
  • Retention favored: thumb involvement, static type, willing family
  • Debulking: mid-lateral, one side at a time; can excise hypertrophied nerves preserving vessels
  • Epiphysiodesis: most dependable method to stop longitudinal growth; performed when digit matches same-sex parent’s length; percutaneous drill at all 3 phalangeal physes

Clinodactyly

  • Observation for most small finger clinodactyly (splinting provides no benefit)
  • Physiolysis (Vickers procedure): open physes, < 6 years; resect bracketed epiphysis + fat graft; mean 82% correction
  • Closing wedge osteotomy: severe deformity; mean correction 33° → 9°

Symphalangism

  • Majority = observation; compensatory hyperflexion at adjacent joints
  • Early joint release (18-24 months) for Flatt type I fibrous/cartilaginous; average 40° improvement

Camptodactyly

  • Nonsurgical mainstay: Hori protocol — splinting 24 hr/day until full extension, then 8 hr/day until skeletal maturity
  • Passive stretching effective in children < 3 years (Rhee: 20° → 1° in mild, 75° → 28° in severe)
  • Surgery: stepwise — volar skin z-plasty → FDS release → NV assessment → volar plate release → collateral release → K-wire

Clasped Thumb

  • Full-time extension splinting initially; surgery if no functional extension by age 2
  • EIP is preferred donor for tendon transfer; palmaris longus alternative
  • First web augmented with Ezaki-Oishi index rotation flap

Trigger Thumb

  • Presents age 1-4; incidence 1-3 per 1,000; believed acquired, not congenital
  • 76% spontaneous resolution at 49 months (Baek); 92% with nighttime splinting at 22 months (Koh)
  • US practice: open A1 pulley release; transverse incision at volar MCP crease
  • FPL runs from center of thumb pulp toward hook of hamate
  • Avoid complete division of oblique pulley (bowstringing)

Trigger Finger (Pediatric)

  • 10x less common than trigger thumb; long finger most commonly affected

  • Often associated with MPS, JRA, Ehlers-Danlos, Down syndrome

  • May require FDS slip resection, A3 release, partial A2 release

  • Recurrence 9% with A1 excision + FDS slip resection

  • Pediatric trigger thumb is acquired (not congenital) and distinct from adult trigger finger; most resolve spontaneously, with reported splinting resolution rates around 92% — splint first, operate later

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Pediatric trigger thumb: most resolve with observation/splinting — don’t rush to surgery
  • Camptodactyly: splinting is the mainstay; surgery risks creating extension contracture (FDS-to-extensor transfers)
  • Delta phalanx = longitudinal epiphyseal bracket; splinting does nothing for clinodactyly
  • Macrodactyly: epiphysiodesis timing = when digit matches parent’s size (most predictable method)
  • CBS: acrosyndactyly release by 6 months; deep bands with ischemia are emergencies

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A 2-year-old presents with the thumb locked in IP flexion and a palpable nodule at the MCP crease. What percentage will resolve with observation? — A: ~76% at 49 months (Baek); 92% with nighttime splinting (Koh).
  2. Q: A child has progressive small finger angulation from a trapezoidal middle phalanx on radiograph. What is the underlying pathology? — A: Delta phalanx (longitudinal epiphyseal bracket) causing asymmetric growth.
  3. Q: What is the most common type of macrodactyly? — A: Type I — lipofibromatosis (“true” macrodactyly).
  4. Q: What is the preferred tendon donor for tendon transfer in congenital clasped thumb? — A: EIP (extensor indicis proprius).
