Session 3 — Tendon & Nerve Injuries

Week 5 of 8

Flexor tendon injuries occupy a unique position in hand surgery owing to their complex zonal anatomy, narrow margin for technical error, and the strong influence of postoperative rehabilitation on functional outcome. Extensor tendon injuries, while seemingly more straightforward, carry their own pitfalls and frequently produce poor results when undertreated. Peripheral nerve injuries — by anatomic distribution — round out the session.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the flexor tendon zones, pulley system, and the surgical approach to a zone II laceration
  • List the Leddy classification of jersey finger and explain why type I is a surgical emergency
  • Name the Doyle extensor zones I-IX and match injury patterns to each (mallet, boutonniere, sagittal band, EPL rupture)
  • Apply the Seddon and Sunderland classifications to a peripheral nerve injury and predict recovery

Questions

Anatomy & classification

  1. Name the 5 flexor tendon zones and state the single most important anatomic feature of each zone for repair.
  2. List the annular (A1-A5) and cruciate (C1-C3) pulleys, and identify which two pulleys are biomechanically most important to preserve.
  3. Classify jersey finger (Leddy). Which type needs repair within 7-10 days and why?
  4. Name the extensor tendon zones (Doyle) for the finger and the thumb. What is the classic injury at each even-numbered zone in the finger?
  5. Compare Seddon and Sunderland classifications. Which Sunderland grades recover spontaneously and which need surgical repair?

Exam maneuvers

  1. How do you distinguish a complete flexor tendon laceration from a partial one on exam? What percent laceration warrants formal repair?
  2. A patient can extend the thumb MCP at rest on a flat table but cannot lift the thumb off the table. Which tendon is out, and what’s the classic setting for this injury?

Clinical vignettes

  1. A 22-year-old football player jams his ring finger grabbing a jersey. Can’t flex the DIP. You see the tendon stump retracted to the palm on ultrasound. What’s the diagnosis, Leddy type, and timeline for repair?
  2. A 35-year-old with a zone II flexor tendon laceration to the long finger 6 hours ago — FDP and FDS both cut. Describe your OR plan: repair technique (core + epitendinous), suture strategy, pulley management, rehab protocol.
  3. A 45-year-old laborer sustains a forearm laceration with complete median nerve transection at the mid-forearm. Repair is done primarily end-to-end with epineurial sutures. Six months later he has no thenar recovery and no volar index sensation. What nerve transfers would you consider to restore thenar function and critical sensation?
  4. A 40-year-old sustains a mallet finger after catching a ball. X-ray shows no fracture. Closed, soft-tissue mallet with 25° extensor lag. Treatment plan and when do you operate?

Answer Key

See templates/flexor-tendon-repair-handout.md for in-depth answers on questions 1-3 and 9. Summary answers:

  1. Flexor zones:

    • Zone I: only FDP (distal to FDS insertion on middle phalanx). Jersey finger territory.
    • Zone II: “no man’s land” — A1 pulley to middle phalanx; both FDS + FDP in a narrow sheath under pulleys. Historically poor outcomes → Bunnell’s original injunction against primary repair; now primary repair is standard with early active motion.
    • Zone III: palm (distal TCL edge to A1) — lumbrical origins; outcomes good.
    • Zone IV: within the carpal tunnel — must protect median nerve, may need TCL release/repair.
    • Zone V: forearm — often with nerve injuries; outcomes good if no nerve involvement.

  2. Pulleys: A1-A5 + C1-C3. A2 and A4 are biomechanically most important — venting one pulley partially is usually OK, but loss of both causes bowstringing. Modern teaching allows partial venting of A2 and A4 to facilitate repair.

  3. Leddy classification (jersey finger, ring finger most common):

    • Type I: tendon retracts to palm (lumbrical origin released) — vincula torn, avascular — repair within 7-10 days or it shortens and cannot be re-advanced.
    • Type II: tendon retracts to PIP — caught on FDS decussation, vincula intact — more time (up to weeks).
    • Type III: large bony fragment caught at A4 — retracts less, more time.
    • Type IV: fracture through the distal phalanx + tendon avulsion off the bony fragment — “double injury” — requires fracture fixation + tendon re-attachment.

  4. Doyle extensor zones (finger / thumb):

    • I / TI: DIP / thumb IP → mallet / EPL avulsion at insertion
    • II / TII: middle phalanx / thumb proximal phalanx
    • III / TIII: PIP / thumb MCP → central slip (boutonniere) / EPB at MCP
    • IV / TIV: proximal phalanx / thumb metacarpal
    • V / TV: MCP → sagittal band rupture (ulnar most common, long finger)
    • VI: dorsum of hand
    • VII: extensor retinaculum (wrist level)
    • VIII: distal forearm
    • IX: muscle bellies

  5. Seddon vs Sunderland:

    • Neurapraxia (I): local conduction block, no Wallerian degeneration → full recovery weeks.
    • Axonotmesis (II-IV):
      • II: axon disrupted, endoneurium intact → recovers via regeneration at 1 mm/day
      • III: axon + endoneurium disrupted, perineurium intact → recovery incomplete
      • IV: axon + endoneurium + perineurium disrupted, epineurium intact → needs surgical repair
    • Neurotmesis (V): complete transection → always needs surgical repair.
    • Grades I-II recover spontaneously; III variable; IV-V need surgery.

  6. Flexor tendon laceration exam + partial repair threshold. Test FDS and FDP in isolation against resistance — if the patient can fully flex but has pain or weakness, suspect a partial tear. Generally repair if > 60% laceration (some say > 50%); <60% can be trimmed and mobilized. Partial tears under the A2 pulley can trigger (catching) and may need debridement even if small.

  7. EPL rupture. Patient can extend the MCP (via EIP/EDC joint-to-joint connection and intrinsics) but cannot retropulse the thumb off a flat table (EPL is the only tendon that lifts the thumb away from the plane of the palm). Classic setting: non-displaced distal radius fracture (attritional rupture at Lister’s tubercle, 6-8 weeks post-injury) OR post-DRF ORIF with dorsally prominent hardware. Treatment: EIP to EPL transfer.

  8. Jersey finger, Leddy type I. Tendon retracted to palm = vincula torn = no intrinsic blood supply, shortening within days. Repair within 7-10 days — after that, the muscle-tendon unit shortens and scars, precluding primary repair → staged reconstruction (silicone rod then tendon graft) or DIP fusion.

  9. Zone II flexor repair. OR plan:

    • WALANT or regional block (WALANT allows intraoperative active motion to test repair)
    • Extend wound with Bruner or mid-lateral incision
    • Preserve A2 and A4 (vent partially if needed — ≤ 25% of A2 or all of A4 venting is acceptable)
    • Identify FDS slips (decussation = Camper’s chiasm) + FDP stump; retrieve proximal tendon with a pediatric feeding tube or tendon retriever if needed — do not grasp the tendon with forceps through the pulley
    • Core suture: 4- or 6-strand repair (Strickland modification, Tang 6-strand) with 3-0 or 4-0 braided suture — multi-strand allows early active motion
    • Epitendinous suture: running 5-0 or 6-0 nylon or Prolene — improves gliding and strength
    • Tension-check with intraoperative WALANT if possible
    • Dorsal blocking splint 30° wrist flexion, 60° MCP flexion, IPs extended
    • Rehab: early active motion (place-and-hold, Strickland or Indiana protocol) starting day 3-5 with hand therapy

  10. Nerve transfers for median nerve injury at mid-forearm with failed recovery.

    • Thenar (motor) restoration: AIN to recurrent motor branch of median is typically done at the time of repair (supercharge), but at 6 months with no recovery → opponensplasty (EIP opposition transfer — Burkhalter) is the reconstructive option
    • Sensation to index/thumb (critical): end-to-side transfer of a digital nerve from the ring to the index/thumb, or ulnar digital nerve of the long to the radial digital nerve of the thumb/index (Mackinnon)
    • Note: once you’re 6 months out with no thenar recovery, nerve transfers for motor are usually too late — motor endplate degeneration — and tendon transfers (opponensplasty) are the workhorse.

  11. Closed soft-tissue mallet. Full-time DIP extension splinting × 6-8 weeks, then night splinting × 2-4 more weeks. Operate only if:

    • Bony mallet with > 30% articular involvement + volar subluxation of the distal phalanx
    • Chronic mallet > 3 months with functional deficit
    • Failed conservative care Patient compliance with splinting is 90% of the battle — counsel aggressively.

  12. b. Vincula longa and brevia from the digital arteries. Synovial diffusion contributes but vincula are primary. This is why restoring vincular continuity is biomechanically preferred when possible.

  13. a. Median nerve at the wrist. Loss of thumb opposition (APB) + palmar sensation of index/long = median at the wrist. Preserved wrist and finger flexion confirms the lesion is distal to the forearm branches. AIN palsy has NO sensory component. Recurrent motor only = no sensory loss.
