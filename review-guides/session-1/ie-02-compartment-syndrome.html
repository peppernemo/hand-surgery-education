Compartment Syndrome of the Upper Extremity

Review Guide — Source: Handthology

Hybrid format: tight board-prep bullets layered with Barrera-voice clinical pearls. Target length: 1-3 pages. Drop unused sections; do NOT pad.

Anatomy / Basic Science

Forearm (Most Common Site)

  • 3 compartments: volar (superficial + deep), dorsal, mobile wad
  • Volar superficial: FDS, PT, PL, FCR, FCU; deep: FDP, FPL, PQ
  • Subcompartments: PQ and FDP often distinct — decompress independently
  • Volar decompression must include lacertus fibrosus proximally + transverse carpal ligament distally

Hand

  • Thenar, hypothenar (2 compartments in 76%), adductor, interossei (2+ compartments per intermetacarpal space)
  • Carpal tunnel is relevant but not a true fascial compartment

Brachium

  • 3 compartments: deltoid (may need anterior/middle/posterior subcompartment decompression), anterior, posterior

Physiology

  • Normal resting pressures: 0-8 mm Hg (forearm), <15 mm Hg (hand interossei)
  • Cascade: increased pressure → venous obstruction → capillary leak → further pressure → decreased arterial inflow → ischemia → necrosis
  • Tissue perfusion ceases when interstitial pressure = diastolic BP (Dahn)
  • Matava: critical threshold = 20 mm Hg below diastolic BP

Classification

  1. Acute incipient: early, pressures at/below threshold; observe closely
  2. Acute established — reversible: <8 hours; reversible with timely decompression
  3. Acute established — irreversible: >8 hours; some irreversible changes
  4. Late established: post-cell death, pre-fibrosis
  5. Volkmann’s ischemic contracture: end result; muscle replaced by fibrous tissue; most often deep volar (FDP, FPL, PQ)
  6. Chronic exertional: transient; resolves with rest

Workup

Clinical — 5 P’s (Griffiths)

  1. Pain (most important; aggravated by passive stretch)
  2. Pallor
  3. Paresthesias
  4. Paresis
  5. Pulselessness (rare; late finding)
  • Sensitivity of individual clinical signs: only 13-19% (Ulmer)
  • Matsen progression: diminished light touch → hypesthesia → motor weakness → anesthesia
  • Children: restlessness, irritability, increasing analgesic need — cannot describe paresthesias

Pressure Measurement

  • Indications: equivocal exam, obtunded patients
  • Diagnostic threshold: absolute >30 mm Hg OR within 30 mm Hg of diastolic BP
  • Stryker handheld manometer most common; side-port needle most accurate
  • Measure at multiple locations — significant variability (pressures higher within 5 cm of fracture)

Barrera: Gustilo classification is for lower extremity only — don’t apply it to open hand or upper-extremity injuries.

Etiology

  • Most common: forearm fractures (18% of all forearm compartment syndromes)
  • Fasciotomy in 1.6% of all forearm fractures; open fracture = 5x higher likelihood
  • Risk factors: male, younger, OTA type C, both-bone forearm, substance abuse
  • Other: reperfusion injury, IV extravasation, drug injection, coagulopathy, “found-down” extremity (increasingly common with drug overdose)

Treatment

Preoperative

  • Limb at or slightly below heart level (improves perfusion via gravity)
  • Remove constrictive bandages/splints
  • Aggressive fluid + BP resuscitation; oxygenation
  • Monitor CPK, urine myoglobin
  • Mannitol diuresis reduces incidence post-revascularization

Surgical

  • Dermatomy must be included — subcutaneous/endoscopic fasciotomy alone is insufficient
  • Do NOT primarily close wounds — cadaver studies show significant pressure rise with skin closure
  • Debride nonviable tissue (definitive extent may not be known until later)

Volar forearm: Henry, lazy “S” (also releases mobile wad), or ulnar-based (Ronel/Matsen); must include lacertus + carpal tunnel

Hand: radial thenar + ulnar hypothenar + 2 longitudinal dorsal incisions (between index-long and ring-small) for interossei; extensile carpal tunnel approach

Wound Management

  • NPWT preferred (often with Jacob’s ladder underneath)
  • Jacob’s ladder / shoestring: vessel loops + skin staples, serial adjustment
  • DermaClose: continuous tension, no serial adjustment needed
  • If 3-4 procedures insufficient → STSG or skin substitute
  • Fasciotomy infection rate: 14% (DM OR 16.5, open fracture OR 5.2)

Complications & Outcomes

  • Delay/missed compartment syndrome = catastrophic outcomes + medicolegal risk
  • Volkmann’s contracture: end-stage fibrotic replacement of muscle — deep volar most commonly affected
  • Variable outcomes dependent on: tissue trauma, time to decompression, pressures, patient health
  • Aggressive postop rehab critical: antiedema, scar desensitization, joint mobilization, early ROM

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Clinical signs have only 13-19% sensitivity individually — maintain high index of suspicion
  • Pain with passive stretch is the earliest and most important sign
  • Pulselessness is a late finding — compartment syndrome is a clinical diagnosis, not a vascular one
  • Measure pressures at multiple locations; highest within 5 cm of fracture
  • Volar forearm decompression is not complete without lacertus fibrosus AND carpal tunnel release
  • Do NOT close fasciotomy wounds primarily — this recreates the compartment syndrome
  • “Found-down” extremity from drug overdose is increasingly common and carries unknown ischemia time
  • Children cannot describe paresthesias — watch for agitation, restlessness, escalating analgesia needs

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: What is the diagnostic pressure threshold for compartment syndrome? — A: Absolute compartment pressure >30 mm Hg OR within 30 mm Hg of diastolic blood pressure.

  2. Q: A patient undergoes forearm fasciotomy for compartment syndrome. What two structures MUST be released in addition to the forearm fascia for complete volar decompression? — A: Lacertus fibrosus proximally and the transverse carpal ligament (carpal tunnel) distally.

  3. Q: What is the end-stage consequence of untreated forearm compartment syndrome, and which muscles are most commonly affected? — A: Volkmann’s ischemic contracture; most commonly affects the deep volar compartment (FDP, FPL, pronator quadratus).

  4. Q: An obtunded trauma patient has a both-bone forearm fracture. Clinical exam is unreliable. What tool is used to measure compartment pressures, and what is the most accurate method? — A: Stryker handheld manometer; side-port needle/slit catheter method is most accurate (Whitesides method is least reliable).

  5. Q: What is the reported sensitivity of individual clinical signs (pain, paresthesias, paresis) for diagnosing compartment syndrome? — A: Only 13-19% — high index of suspicion is essential, and no single sign can rule out compartment syndrome.
