Radial Nerve Compression

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Radial nerve enters the arm posteriorly via spiral groove at junction of middle/distal thirds of humerus
  • Enters radial tunnel between brachialis (medial) and brachioradialis (proximal/lateral), ECRL (distal/lateral)
  • Floor: anterior radiocapitellar capsule + deep supinator; roof: fibrous connection brachialis-brachioradialis
  • Bifurcates at radial tunnel exit into SRN (sensory) and PIN (motor)
  • PIN dives under arcade of Frohse (proximal edge of supinator) — common compression site
  • Leash of Henry (radial recurrent artery branches) = most common compressor in radial tunnel
  • SRN becomes subcutaneous 9 cm proximal to radial styloid between ECRL tendon and brachioradialis; bifurcates ~5 cm proximal to styloid
  • Nerves stretched > 6% of length → detectable conduction block from altered perfusion

Classification

  • Saturday night palsy / crutch palsy / honeymoon palsy: proximal compression in spiral groove → neurapraxia; complete wrist/finger/thumb drop
  • Radial tunnel syndrome: pain condition; radial nerve compressed within mobile wad
  • PIN syndrome: motor compression at arcade of Frohse → finger/thumb extension weakness/palsy
  • Wartenberg’s syndrome: SRN compression at brachioradialis exit → dorsoradial forearm/1st webspace numbness

Workup

History

  • Saturday night palsy: acute wrist drop after prolonged arm compression (avg 180 min); worse with alcohol, opioids, DM, hypothyroidism, obesity
  • Radial tunnel: aching mobile wad pain ≥ 2 cm distal to lateral epicondyle; worse with activity; no numbness or weakness; occupational risk = repetitive pronation/pinching
  • PIN syndrome: finger/thumb weakness or extension lag; wrist deviates radially (ECRL intact, ECRB weakened); may have 25% traumatic etiology
  • Wartenberg’s: numbness/paresthesia dorsoradial forearm and 1st webspace; onset with repetitive pronation; coexists with de Quervain’s in up to 50%

Exam

  • Saturday night palsy: complete wrist/finger/thumb extension palsy ± dorsal hand paresthesia; distinguish from PIN (BR + ECRL preserved in PIN)
  • Radial tunnel: tenderness ≥ 2 cm distal to lateral epicondyle (vs epicondylitis = at epicondyle); pain with resisted middle finger extension + resisted supination; no weakness; Rule of Nine test (Loh)
  • PIN syndrome: finger/thumb extension weakness; wrist extension in radial deviation (ECRL unopposed); SBRN sensation intact
  • Wartenberg’s: Tinel over SRN; decreased 2PD in 1st webspace; symptoms with pronation + ulnar deviation; diagnostic lidocaine injection at brachioradialis exit

Electrodiagnostics

  • Saturday night palsy: decreased NCV + amplitude (conduction block); serial EMG — brachioradialis recovers first, EIP last
  • Radial tunnel: typically normal (pain syndrome); dynamic testing may show conduction block with active supination
  • PIN syndrome: motor NCV decreased across supinator; EMG shows denervation of extensor muscles; sensory radial nerve normal
  • Wartenberg’s: variable — normal to complete SRN conduction block; no motor changes

Imaging

  • XR: fracture, malunion, osseous lesion (all syndromes)
  • MRI for PIN: denervation edema/atrophy of supinator/extensors; identify lipomas, Schwannomas, synovitis

Treatment

Saturday Night Palsy (Non-operative)

  • Observation: 89-94% spontaneous recovery (begins avg 2.4 weeks; complete by avg 3.4 months)
  • Cock-up wrist splint + dynamic outriggers; maintain ROM with therapy/nerve gliding
  • Surgery only if no clinical/EDX recovery by 12 weeks (atraumatic) or 3-6 months (traumatic)
  • Open humeral shaft fractures with radial palsy → early nerve exploration (lower spontaneous recovery rate ~85%)

Radial Tunnel Syndrome

  • Non-operative first: activity modification, elbow flexion + supination splinting, nerve glides, stretching; steroid injection (Sarhadi: 64% long-term relief)

  • Operative (failed 3-6 months): extensile anterolateral approach; release radial nerve through radial tunnel, ligate Leash of Henry, release arcade of Frohse; do not over-dissect SRN beyond tunnel

  • Outcomes: 67-92% success in systematic review; worse with concurrent lateral epicondylitis or workers’ comp

  • Diagnostic pearl: localize tenderness precisely — epicondyle vs 2+ cm distal. A diagnostic lidocaine injection into the radial tunnel that relieves symptoms is the most helpful confirmatory test (warn the patient about transient finger/thumb drop)

PIN Syndrome

  • Non-operative if no EDX denervation: activity modification, therapy, serial EDX monitoring
  • Operative if progressive weakness or EMG denervation: full PIN decompression through supinator including distal exit; release arcade of Frohse + distal fibrous edge
  • 3-month rule: if no improvement at 3 months → proceed to decompression (85% good/excellent outcomes); ages > 50 do poorly — consider concomitant tendon transfer (Ochi)
  • Spontaneous PIN recovery is rare; early intervention > conservative treatment

Wartenberg’s Syndrome

  • Non-operative: desensitization therapy, nerve gliding, remove compressive straps/watches; NSAIDS, rest
  • Operative (failed 3-6 months): longitudinal incision 6-8 cm proximal to radial styloid; release brachioradialis over SRN + overlying fascia; passive wrist manipulation to identify additional compression
  • Outcomes: 71% improve non-operatively; surgical success ~75-86%

Complications & Outcomes

  • Saturday night palsy: excellent prognosis; less favorable with older age, EMG denervation, profound initial weakness
  • Radial tunnel surgery: De Smet reported only 40% satisfaction; concurrent epicondylitis and workers’ comp = worse outcomes
  • PIN decompression: 24/25 recovered motor function (Hashizume); avg 5.6 months to recovery; 1 patient required tendon transfer
  • Wartenberg’s: most improve without surgery; surgical outcomes 75% success (Lanzetta)

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Radial tunnel syndrome = a pain diagnosis; PIN syndrome = a motor diagnosis — exam distinguishes them
  • BR + ECRL preserved → PIN palsy (not high radial); wrist extends with radial deviation
  • Saturday night palsy almost always recovers — resist early surgery unless open fracture or penetrating injury
  • Radial tunnel surgery without a confident diagnosis has high failure rates — exhaust non-operative care
  • Wartenberg’s and de Quervain’s coexist in up to 50% — evaluate for both when either is suspected
  • Reinnervation order after proximal radial injury: BR → ECRL → ECRB → EDC → ECU → EDQ → APL → EPL → EPB → EIP

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A patient with lateral elbow pain has tenderness 3 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, pain with resisted middle finger extension, and normal grip strength. EDX is normal. What is the most likely diagnosis? — A: Radial tunnel syndrome; tenderness distal to the epicondyle + pain with resisted middle finger extension + normal EDX distinguishes it from lateral epicondylitis and PIN syndrome.
  2. Q: A patient with PIN palsy extends the wrist in radial deviation. Why? — A: ECRL is innervated by the radial nerve proximal to the PIN takeoff and is preserved; ECRB (mixed radial/PIN innervation) is weakened, leaving unopposed radial deviation.
  3. Q: What is the most common structure causing compression of the radial nerve within the radial tunnel? — A: Leash of Henry (branches of the radial recurrent artery).
  4. Q: A 55-year-old presents with progressive finger drop, EMG denervation of extensor muscles, and a palpable mass at the proximal forearm. What is the next step? — A: MRI to evaluate for space-occupying lesion (lipoma, ganglion, Schwannoma); if mass confirmed, surgical excision + PIN decompression.
