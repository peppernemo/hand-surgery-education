Free Tissue Transfer for Upper Extremity

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Most common cause of UE soft tissue defects: trauma
  • Vital structures superficial in UE → commonly exposed
  • Goal: preserve/restore function, not just wound healing
  • Reconstructive elevator (Gottlieb + Krieger) > ladder: choose the most appropriate option, even if more complex

Indications

  • Exposed functional structures (tendon, bone, nerve, vessels, hardware)
  • Prevent contracture over joints with large defects
  • Tissue to allow tendon glide for future tenolysis
  • Poor intrinsic healing (radiation, chronic infection)
  • Free flap specifically when local options unavailable or create more morbidity

Workup

  • Plain XR essential; CT for complex wrist; angiography rarely needed acutely
  • Clinical exam + surgical exploration usually sufficient for vascular status
  • Timed Allen test important; CT/MR angiography “insufficient” for palmar arch assessment
  • 15% have incomplete superficial palmar arch — dependent on both radial + ulnar
  • Verify perfusion to each digit (clamp + Doppler) before dividing recipient vessel

Treatment

Timing (Godina’s Landmark)

  • <72 hours: infection 1.5%, flap failure <1%
  • Common practice: reconstruction within 5 days of trauma
  • Optimal timing = once debridement is definitive + no suspicion of deeper contamination

Muscle vs Fasciocutaneous Selection

Muscle Fasciocutaneous
Infection No clinical difference (despite animal 10x bacterial control) Same
Contour Thinner after atrophy Difficult to contour primarily
Tendon glide Adheres to bed → may restrict Better long-term flexibility
Re-elevation Difficult Easier for secondary reconstruction
Best use Complex cavitary wounds Over joints/tendons needing glide

Key Free Flaps

ALT (Anterolateral Thigh) — workhorse for large thin coverage: - Pedicle: LCFA descending branch (8-12 cm, 2-2.5 mm) - Up to 15 x 40 cm; primary closure ≤8 cm width - Flow-through option for combined vascular reconstruction - 84% musculocutaneous perforators (intramuscular dissection required)

Lateral Arm: thin, pliable; can be innervated (lower lateral brachial cutaneous nerve); very short pedicle

Radial Forearm (free): thin, pliable, long pedicle (10-15 cm); sacrifices radial artery; visible donor scar; can include LABC nerve for sensate flap

Scapular/Parascapular: can be osteocutaneous; chimeric with latissimus or serratus on subscapular system; short pedicle, requires lateral positioning

Groin/SCIP: historically first free flap; short narrow pedicle; technically challenging thin elevation

Latissimus Dorsi: largest single muscle; pedicle = thoracodorsal (6-8 cm, 2-2.5 mm; up to 12 cm if dissected to subscapular); chimeric with serratus ± scapular bone ± ribs

Gracilis: minimal donor morbidity; functional muscle transfer for elbow/finger flexion (10 cm excursion); short pedicle (5-6 cm); thin-walled vessels easily damaged by traction

Vascularized Fibula: up to 30 cm bone; pedicle = peroneal artery (5-6 cm, 2.5 mm); preserve 6-7 cm fibula distally for ankle stability

MFC/Trochlea Flap: pedicle = DGA (7-9 cm, 1.5 mm); corticoperiosteal, corticocancellous, or osteocartilaginous; best for nonunions throughout body; limited to bone defects <5 cm

Recipient Vessels

Arteries: brachial (end-to-side OK), antecubital bifurcation (end-to-end), distal ulnar (workhorse), radial in forearm or snuffbox (confirm Allen test)

Veins: cephalic (superficial radial), basilic (superficial ulnar), deep comitantes; antecubital fossa has abundance

Complications & Outcomes

  • Vascular thrombosis <5% in experienced centers — most devastating complication
  • Inset critical: no tension/compression on pedicle; “comfortable” pedicle course
  • Muscle flaps: continuous implantable Doppler monitoring recommended (hard to assess clinically)
  • Fasciocutaneous: pencil Doppler + clinical exam typically sufficient

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Elevator > ladder in the upper extremity — function, not just wound healing
  • Godina: <72 hours = best outcomes (1.5% infection, <1% failure)
  • ALT is the workhorse for large defects; gracilis for functional muscle transfer
  • Fasciocutaneous flaps are preferred over joints/tendons (better glide, easier re-elevation)
  • MFC flap is the go-to for nonunions with small bone defects
  • 15% of patients have incomplete palmar arch — always verify before dividing recipient
  • Don’t use contralateral uninjured forearm as donor (radial forearm free flap)

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: According to Godina’s landmark study, what are the infection and flap failure rates when free tissue transfer is performed within 72 hours of injury? — A: 1.5% infection, <1% flap failure.

  2. Q: What flap provides the best long-term tendon gliding for coverage over the dorsal hand? — A: Fasciocutaneous flap (e.g., ALT) — better flexibility and gliding than muscle flaps, and easier to re-elevate for secondary reconstruction.

  3. Q: What is the maximum amount of fibula that can be harvested as a vascularized free flap? — A: Up to 30 cm; must preserve 6-7 cm of distal fibula for ankle stability.

  4. Q: What is the workhorse free flap for functional muscle transfer to restore finger flexion? — A: Gracilis — minimal donor morbidity, 10 cm excursion, can include a skin paddle for monitoring.
