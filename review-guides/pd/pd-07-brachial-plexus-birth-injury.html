Brachial Plexus Birth Injury

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Brachial plexus: C5-T1 ventral rami; variations with C4 (prefixed, more common) or T2 (postfixed) in up to 50%
  • Components: roots → trunks → divisions → cords → branches
  • Erb’s point: C5 + C6 converge to form upper trunk; just lateral/superior to SCM insertion on clavicle
  • Phrenic nerve on anterior surface of anterior scalene → key surgical landmark guiding to C5 root
  • Posterior cord absent in up to 20% of patients (anatomic variation)

Epidemiology

  • ~1.5 per 1,000 live births; right > left (left occiput anterior presentation)
  • 50% isolated C5-C6; 30% C5-C7; 20% C5-T1 (global)
  • Risk factors: shoulder dystocia (OR = 138.7), prior BPBI, macrosomia > 4,000 g, instrumented delivery
  • Up to 50% have no known risk factors
  • Spontaneous recovery in 66-82%; ~30% have persistent deficits

Classification

Narakas Classification

Group Roots Presentation Prognosis
1 (Erb’s) C5-C6 Absent shoulder abd/elbow flex, weak wrist ext, hand OK Best
2 (Extended Erb’s) C5-C7 “Waiter’s tip”: shoulder IR, elbow ext, forearm pronation, wrist drop 2A: early wrist recovery; 2B: late/absent
3 (Global) C5-T1 Flail limb Poor
4 (Global + Horner) C5-T1 Flail + ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis Worst — often avulsions

Nerve Injury Severity (Seddon/Sunderland)

  • Neurapraxia (I): demyelination only → full spontaneous recovery
  • Axonotmesis (II-IV): variable recovery depending on endoneurium/perineurium/epineurium injury
  • Neurotmesis (V): complete disruption → no spontaneous recovery, requires surgery

Pre- vs Post-Ganglionic

  • Pre-ganglionic (avulsion): poor prognosis; clinical signs = Horner’s, elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic), scapular winging (long thoracic), weak rhomboids (dorsal scapular)
  • Post-ganglionic: better potential for spontaneous recovery

Glenohumeral Dysplasia — Waters Classification

  • Type I-VII, from < 5° retroversion difference (I) to proximal humeral growth arrest (VII)

Workup

Infant Evaluation

  • Detailed birth history: dystocia, instrumentation, birth weight
  • DDx: clavicle/humerus fracture, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, cerebral palsy, arthrogryposis
  • Examine: torticollis, Horner’s, asymmetric chest wall motion (phrenic), scapular winging, limb posture
  • Active Movement Scale (AMS): 15 motions, 8-point scale
  • Toronto Test: elbow flex + elbow/wrist/finger/thumb extension; < 3.5 at 3 months → may indicate surgery
  • Passive shoulder ER ≤ 60° → prompt radiographic evaluation (glenohumeral dysplasia)

Imaging

  • XR: clavicle/humerus fractures, chest for hemidiaphragm
  • US/MRI: glenohumeral congruency, glenoid version, humeral head deformity
  • EMG/NCS: less reliable in BPBI (underestimates injury, overestimates recovery)

Treatment

Nonsurgical

  • All infants start with nonsurgical care: stretching and ROM (emphasis on glenohumeral abduction/ER with scapular stabilization)

Operative Indications

Age Indication
3 months Flail + Horner; Toronto < 3.5
6 months No antigravity elbow flexion (AMS < 5); Toronto not improving
9 months Failure of “cookie test”

Microsurgical Reconstruction — Nerve Grafting

  • Supraclavicular transverse approach; identify phrenic → C5 → trace to Erb’s neuroma
  • Sural nerve cable grafts at 110% defect length; 9-0 or 10-0 nylon + fibrin glue
  • C5-C6: prioritize shoulder ER/abd, elbow flexion, wrist extension
  • Global: prioritize hand function first, then elbow flexion, then shoulder

Nerve Transfers

Transfer Restores
SAN → suprascapular Shoulder ER/abd
Oberlin (ulnar → biceps MC) Elbow flexion
Radial (triceps branch) → axillary Shoulder abd
Median → brachialis Elbow flexion
Intercostal → musculocutaneous Elbow flexion (global palsies)
  • Advantage over grafting: avoids scar dissection, closer to motor endplates
  • Evidence favors SAN → suprascapular nerve transfer

Secondary Shoulder Reconstruction

  • Internal rotation contracture in 8-29% of infants; glenohumeral dysplasia in 30% of older children
  • Mild dysplasia: anterior musculotendinous lengthening + lat dorsi/teres major transfer to rotator cuff (90° abd, 60-80° ER)
  • Moderate dysplasia: add open glenohumeral reduction + capsulorraphy
  • Severe dysplasia: external rotation humeral osteotomy (deltopectoral, 45-70° rotation; most feared complication = overcorrection with loss of midline function)

Other Sequelae

  • Elbow flexion contracture (~50%): nighttime splinting for 20-40°; serial casting for ≥ 40°

  • Forearm supination contracture (7%): biceps rerouting if passively correctable; osteotomy if fixed

  • BPBI surgical decision tree pivots on antigravity elbow flexion: absent by 6 months → microsurgical reconstruction indicated

  • Horner’s syndrome at birth with a flail limb implies avulsions — proceed early (at ~3 months) rather than waiting

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Antigravity elbow flexion by 3 months = excellent prognosis; absent by 6 months = surgery indicated
  • Horner’s syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) = pre-ganglionic avulsion, worst prognosis → early surgery at 3 months
  • Phrenic nerve on anterior scalene = the surgical landmark to C5
  • EMG/NCS unreliable in infants — serial clinical exam is the gold standard
  • Global palsy reconstruction: prioritize hand function over shoulder
  • Passive shoulder ER ≤ 60° → image the glenohumeral joint for dysplasia
  • External rotation osteotomy: fear overcorrection (iatrogenic ER contracture, loss of midline function)

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A 6-month-old with BPBI cannot flex the elbow against gravity. What is the recommended next step? — A: Microsurgical brachial plexus reconstruction (neuroma excision + nerve grafting or nerve transfer).
  2. Q: A neonate with a flail arm has ipsilateral ptosis and miosis. What does this indicate about the injury? — A: Pre-ganglionic nerve root avulsions (Horner syndrome from sympathetic chain involvement) — Narakas group 4 with the worst prognosis.
  3. Q: What nerve serves as the intraoperative landmark to locate the C5 root during brachial plexus exploration? — A: The phrenic nerve, on the anterior surface of the anterior scalene muscle.
  4. Q: In the Oberlin transfer, what is the donor nerve and what function is restored? — A: A fascicle of the ulnar nerve is transferred to the biceps branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, restoring elbow flexion.
  5. Q: A child with BPBI has passive shoulder external rotation of 40°. What evaluation is needed? — A: Imaging (US or MRI) to assess glenohumeral congruency and glenoid version for dysplasia.
