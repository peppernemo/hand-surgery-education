Tetraplegia and Stroke

Anatomy / Basic Science

UMN vs LMN

Feature UMN LMN
Pattern Myotome groups Individual muscles
Atrophy Mild (disuse) Pronounced (70-80%)
Tone Spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski Flaccid, hypotonia, decreased reflexes
Fasciculations Absent May be present
EMG/NCS Normal NCS, no denervation Abnormal NCS, denervation

Zone of Injury (Coulet)

  • Three-dimensional zone centered on but not confined to cord level
  • Above zone: normal; below zone: UMN (hyperreflexia); within zone: mixed UMN/LMN
  • Nerve-root avulsion or anterior horn cell injury → pure LMN
  • Larger zones → more contractures + worse forearm/hand posturing
  • Forearm supination contracture risk correlates with C5-segment zone size

Epidemiology

  • ~54/million annual incidence (~17,500/yr US); 285,000 Americans with SCI
  • C5 = most common level (15.2%); C4 + C6 next
  • 54.2% tetraplegic (most incomplete)
  • Hand function = greatest functional loss cited by 76% of patients
  • First-year cost $780K; lifetime $3.5M (low-level tetraplegia, young patient)
  • Only 12-14% of candidates undergo upper limb reconstruction

Classification — ICSHT (International Classification for Surgery of the Hand in Tetraplegia)

Group Grade 4 Muscle Key Implication
0 None No tendon transfers; FES research
1 BR Elbow ext + wrist ext + tenodesis pinch
2 ECRL Elbow ext + active lateral pinch
3 ECRB + Palmar grasp possible
4 PT Lateral pinch + palmar grasp
5 FCR May not need elbow ext
6 EDC Same as above
7 EPL Single stage; intrinsic restoration
8+ Finger flexors Individualized
T+/T- Triceps grade 4
  • ICSHT number = potential donor muscles

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Joint suppleness, contracture prevention
  • Spasmolytics (oral, intrathecal, botulinum toxin)
  • TES (transcutaneous electrical stimulation): converts type II glycolytic → slow oxidative fibers (fatigue resistant)
  • Splinting matched to level (C1-C4 resting hand → C6 wrist-drive flexor hinge → C7 MCP block)

Operative — Goal Priority Order

  1. Elbow extension — workspace + stabilizes distal transfers
  2. Wrist extension — foundation for tenodesis pinch/grasp
  3. Lateral pinch + release — most-used grasp pattern
  4. Palmar grasp + release — larger objects

Tendon Transfer Principles

  • Supple joints with functional passive ROM
  • Donor MMT ≥ 4 (loses 1 grade with transfer)
  • Comparable excursion + force; synergy facilitates re-education
  • Straight line of pull; minimal donor morbidity
  • Never sacrifice ECRB unless 2+ wrist extensors strong
  • ECRL preferred donor when 2 wrist extensors available

Coaptation

  • Modified side-to-side (Brown) — stronger, lower profile, allows early mobilization vs Pulvertaft weave

  • Tendon transfer loses one grade of strength — a grade 4 donor becomes grade 3 at best (antigravity only). Donors at grade 3 are not candidates.

Elbow Extension

Posterior Deltoid to Triceps

  • Posterior 1/3 deltoid + central 1/3 triceps turn-up graft
  • Long-arm cast 20° flex × 3-4 wk → hinged orthosis, progressive flexion 15°/wk
  • Average MMT 3 achieved

Biceps to Triceps (Kuz/Revol)

  • Brachialis = primary elbow flexor; biceps can be spared
  • Route medially under ulnar nerve, weave into triceps + olecranon bone tunnel
  • Re-educate via forearm supination (synergy)
  • Rupture rate up to 14% (early mobilization + fluoroquinolones)
  • ~47% flexion strength loss but acceptable

Lateral Pinch — 4 Components

  1. Thumb IP stabilization — split FPL transfer (radial half FPL → EPL)
  2. CMC stabilization — arthrodesis (40° palmar abd, 25° ext, 10° pronation) or adductor-opponensplasty
  3. FPL activation — BR → FPL (workhorse); set tension: pinch at 0-10° wrist extension
  4. Extensor tenodesis if needed (EPL/APL lasso at retinaculum)

Palmar Grasp

  • ECRL → synchronized FDP (“reverse cascade”) for finger flexion
  • Set tension: fingers flex with wrist 30-45° extended
  • Intrinsic reconstruction: Zancolli FDS lasso (FDS → A1 pulley, MCP flexion with wrist extension) or House lateral band graft

Contracture Management

  • Forearm supination contracture → radial pronation osteotomy (transverse midshaft, rotate 10-20° pronation, locking plate)
  • Elbow contractures: serial casting first; surgical for > 50° (triceps strong) or > 25° (no triceps)
  • Intrinsic tightness: selective ulnar-sided intrinsic release before any reconstruction

Nerve Transfers

  • Alternative to tendon transfer; single donor → multiple muscles
  • Supinator branch → PIN (finger + thumb extension) — most common
  • Fox study: 73% achieve M4+ recovery; minimal donor deficit
  • Advantages: no immobilization, less intensive therapy
  • Disadvantages: 6 mo-2 yr for strength; failure 10-40% (vs near 0% for tendon transfer rupture)
  • Time-sensitive: ~18-21 mo window before chronic denervation

Outcomes

  • 80-83% good-excellent functional gains at 7+ yr (Lamb/Chan)
  • 75% gain self-catheterization after pinch reconstruction
  • 81-90% would have surgery again
  • FES: 87% general satisfaction, 88% life improvement

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Hand function is the #1 priority for tetraplegic patients — yet only 12-14% get reconstruction
  • ICSHT classification drives surgical planning — know donor muscles by group
  • Elbow extension first, then wrist, then pinch — proximal stability before distal finesse
  • Never sacrifice ECRB unless 2+ wrist extensors are strong
  • BR = workhorse donor; mobilize fully for 78 mm excursion (native only 42 mm)
  • Biceps-to-triceps: counsel about 14% rupture rate and 47% flexion loss
  • Nerve transfers offer an alternative but have higher failure rates and longer recovery

Board-Style Questions

  1. Q: What is the most common cervical level of spinal cord injury? A: C5 (15.2%).
  2. Q: In ICSHT classification, what does the group number represent? A: The number of muscles available at grade 4 strength as potential tendon transfer donors.
  3. Q: What is the priority order for surgical reconstruction in tetraplegia? A: Elbow extension → wrist extension → lateral pinch/release → palmar grasp/release.
  4. Q: What nerve transfer restores finger and thumb extension in tetraplegia? A: Supinator branch to posterior interosseous nerve (PIN).
  5. Q: What percentage of candidates for upper limb reconstruction actually undergo surgery? A: Only 12-14%.
