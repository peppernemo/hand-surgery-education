Topic 8 · Infection & Tumor

FTS · fight bite · deep space infections · compartment syndrome · nec fasc · ganglion, giant cell tumor & the rest

This is most of the overnight consult pager plus the two masses you will see weekly in clinic. The infection diagnoses are made at the bedside and delay costs function; the tumor questions are pattern recognition.

Interns own: the four Kanavel signs, the fight-bite rule (OR, no matter how small the wound), and the compartment syndrome exam.

Test yourself

  1. The four Kanavel signs, the most specific one, and the admission bundle for flexor tenosynovitis (FTS) at JHMC.
  2. A 32-year-old punched a tooth 18 hours ago: 3 mm dorsal MCP wound, pain with flexion. Why does this go to the OR, and on what antibiotics?
  3. Differentiate felon, paronychia, and herpetic whitlow, and name the one you must never incise.
  4. Crush injury, pain out of proportion, worse with passive finger extension, 6 hours in. When do pressures matter, what are the numbers, and which volar incision does this practice draw?
  5. Rapidly spreading erythema, dusky skin, crepitus, LRINEC 9. What happens in the next 60 minutes?
  6. A wrist mass transilluminates and appeared over weeks; a separate patient has a firm, lobulated, non-mobile volar finger mass that does not transilluminate. Diagnoses, workups, and recurrence rates after excision.
  7. A hand series shows a well-defined lytic lesion in the proximal phalanx found after a trivial fracture. Most likely diagnosis and management? What imaging feature would instead worry you?
Answers
  1. Fusiform swelling, flexed posture, tenderness along the sheath, pain with passive extension (most specific). Bundle: NPO at midnight, IV vancomycin + Unasyn, elevation, Toradol, consent for sheath irrigation with possible open washout; very early cases (<24 h) can occasionally trial IV antibiotics with serial exams by the surgical team. FTS is a surgical urgency.
  2. The tooth violates the extensor hood and usually the MCP joint with the fist clenched; the tracts seal at rest, so the benign look lies. X-ray for tooth fragment/air/fracture, ampicillin-sulbactam (covers Eikenella), OR arthrotomy and irrigation, wound left open or loose, early motion once clean.
  3. Felon: pulp abscess in the fibrous septa; lateral or distal volar midline I&D, never a fishmouth. Paronychia: nail-fold infection; lift the fold or remove part of the nail; soaks early. Herpetic whitlow: HSV vesicles; do NOT incise (superinfection, prolonged shedding); acyclovir and a dry dressing.
  4. The exam is the diagnosis: pain out of proportion plus passive stretch pain is enough for the OR. Pressures are for the obtunded or equivocal: delta pressure (diastolic minus compartment) under 30 mmHg, or absolute over 30-45. Volar approach here is ulnar-based (preserves the radial forearm flap donor site), plus carpal tunnel extension, dorsal, mobile wad, and hand releases as indicated; leave open, relook at 48 hours.
  5. Necrotizing fasciitis: resuscitate, broad antibiotics (vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin for toxin suppression), and book the OR for wide debridement without waiting for imaging. Serial debridements every 24-48 hours. The diagnosis is clinical and surgical.
  6. First: dorsal wrist ganglion from the scapholunate interval; transilluminates; aspiration recurs 50%+, excision including the stalk recurs ~5-15% (volar ganglions ride the radial artery: Allen’s first). Second: giant cell tumor of tendon sheath: second most common hand mass, solid, does not transilluminate; excise with marginal margins and warn of ~10-30% recurrence.
  7. Enchondroma: the most common primary bone tumor of the hand; lytic, well-defined, often found via pathologic fracture. Let the fracture heal, then curettage and grafting if symptomatic or structurally weak. Worry features: cortical destruction, soft-tissue mass, pain without fracture, growth: think chondrosarcoma (rare in the hand) and get advanced imaging plus referral instead of curetting.

Friday group case
