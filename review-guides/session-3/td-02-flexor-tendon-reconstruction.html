Flexor Tendon Reconstruction

Classification

Boyes Preoperative Classification:

Grade Condition Implication
I Good — minimal scar, mobile joints Single-stage candidate
II Notable scar, mild contracture Likely two-stage
III Joint damage, decreased ROM Two-stage
IV Nerve damage Two-stage
V Multiple systems (II+III+IV) Two-stage

Workup

History

  • Prior repair details, timing, mechanism, hand dominance, functional demands
  • Document TAM, TAF, TAE, DPC (active + passive)
  • TAM “normal” = 260° (MCP included) or 175° (PIP + DIP only)
  • Assess digital cascade with wrist flex/ext

Imaging

  • XR for arthritis, fracture, deformity
  • MRI/US with caution — pseudotendon can be misread as intact tendon

Patient Selection

  • ≥ 1 uncompromised digital nerve required
  • Contraindications: NV impairment, intractable flexion contractures, noncompliance
  • Preoperative aggressive therapy program — improves motion AND tests compliance
  • Discuss alternatives: arthrodesis, amputation, transfer, tenodesis, no surgery

Treatment

Operative — Single-Stage Reconstruction

Indications: Intact flexor sheath + pulley system, flexible digit, failed primary repair or delayed presentation

  • Both FDS + FDP injured → reconstruct FDP only
  • Intact FDS + functional PIP → reconstructing FDP may do more harm (except young high-demand 10-21 yo)
  • Pass graft via pediatric feeding tube or tendon passer
  • Distal fixation: direct suture to remnant preferred (Tang: 10-12 strands 4-0 nylon); alternatives = pullout button, bone tunnel, screw, suture anchor
  • Proximal: Pulvertaft weave (≥ 2-3 weaves + cross-stitching) in palm or distal forearm
  • Secure distal first → easier wound closure before flexion
  • Tensioning: restore digital cascade; slightly tighter (stretches over time); gradually more flexion index → small

Operative — Two-Stage Reconstruction (Hunter)

Indications: Substantial sheath disruption, severe trauma/crush, failed primary repair or grafting

Stage 1: - Silicone rod implant (4 mm typical) creates pseudosheath (mesothelial cell-lined) - Anchor distally to ≥ 1 cm FDP remnant if possible - Therapy: wound healing, reduce inflammation, minimal motion (excess → synovitis) - Infection rate ~15%

Stage 2 (8-16 wk, later preferable): - Pull rod from distal end → pass tendon graft through pseudosheath - Slight extra tension for anticipated loosening - Pulvertaft weave proximally

Modified Paneva-Holevich (preferred by some): - Stage 1: rod placed + FDS/FDP sutured as loop in palm - Stage 2: rod removed, pedicled FDS flap used as graft — no additional donor tendon needed; one side already healed

  • WALANT preferred for flexor tendon reconstruction — enables intraop active motion assessment, real-time tensioning, and patient education

Barrera: For tumor or infection cases, skip Esmarch exsanguination — elevate only.

Pulley Reconstruction

Indications: Severe bowstringing, multiple pulley injury, flexion lag; full passive motion via therapy first

  • Reconstruct A2 + A4 minimum (± A3)
  • Best graft material: extensor retinaculum (pre-existing synovial gliding surface)
  • Harvest proximal to Lister’s tubercle (more robust)
  • A2 reconstruction: graft under extensor tendon; A4: graft over conjoined extensor
  • Techniques: Bunnell (1 wrap), Lister (least resistance), Karev belt-loop (strongest but decreases ROM)
  • Avoid bone drill holes (phalangeal fracture risk)
  • Postop: external pulley ring bolster × 4-6 weeks

Tenolysis

  • Best ≥ 3 months and ≤ 1 year post-healing
  • Prerequisite: full passive but limited active motion; therapy plateau
  • Wide-awake surgery preferred (real-time active motion assessment)
  • Results: 50-70° TAM improvement in 80% of patients; 20% no gain or loss
  • Start therapy within first few days postop — most motion achieved in first week

Tendon Graft Options

Graft Type Absent % Length
Palmaris longus Extrasynovial 25% 16 cm
Plantaris Extrasynovial 19% 35 cm
EDL 2nd toe Extrasynovial < 1% 35 cm
FDL 2nd toe Intrasynovial < 1% 12 cm
  • Intrasynovial biologically superior (fewer adhesions) but clinical evidence not definitive
  • Palmaris + plantaris most common (minimal donor morbidity)
  • Suitable graft found in 80-85% of patients

Complications & Outcomes

  • Tendon rupture ~7%
  • PIP motion loss: ~30% lose ≥ 30°
  • Need for tenolysis after reconstruction: 12-47%
  • Quadriga: graft too tight / distal adhesions → all FDPs limited (single muscle belly)
  • Paradoxical extension: graft too loose → lumbricals drive IP extension
  • DIP/PIP flexion deformity (8-55°) nearly universal
  • Wide-awake tensioning helps avoid Quadriga and paradoxical extension

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Hierarchy: primary repair > tenolysis > single-stage graft > two-stage (final attempt)
  • Intact FDS with functional PIP — think twice before reconstructing FDP in low-demand patients
  • Preserve ≥ 3 pulleys for single-stage; A2 + A4 most important
  • Modified Paneva-Holevich avoids a separate donor tendon harvest
  • Children: wait until ≥ 6 yo; avoid bone tunnels (physis); modified Paneva-Holevich preferred

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A Boyes grade III patient with joint damage and decreased ROM — is this a single-stage or two-stage reconstruction candidate? — A: Two-stage; single-stage requires Boyes grade I (good tissue, mobile joints, minimal scar).

  2. Q: During single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction, should the graft be secured distally or proximally first, and why? — A: Distal first — easier finger wound closure before the digit is flexed by proximal tensioning.

  3. Q: What complication results from a flexor tendon graft that is tensioned too tight? — A: Quadriga — the tight graft limits excursion of all FDPs (shared muscle belly), reducing adjacent digit flexion.

  4. Q: What is the ideal time window for tenolysis after flexor tendon reconstruction? — A: ≥ 3 months and ≤ 1 year post-healing, once the patient has plateaued in therapy with full passive but limited active motion.
