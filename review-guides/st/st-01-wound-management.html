Wound Management

Classification

  • Open wound: exposed raw tissues from disrupted skin cover
  • Closed wound: skin cover restored (e.g., sutured)
  • Clean wound: all exposed tissues alive, no eschar/debris/smell; can be closed
  • Contaminated wound: dead tissue, pus, foreign debris, or bad smell; cannot be closed until converted to clean
  • Subcategories: acute vs chronic, with vs without dead tissue

Treatment

Timing of Closure

  • Old dogma: close within 12-24 hours
  • Current evidence: cleanliness matters more than timing — routinely close wounds open >24 hours if clean
  • Plastic surgeons close chronic open clean wounds with imported flaps regardless of duration

Transcutaneous vs Buried Sutures

  • Transcutaneous sutures create 2 holes through skin into fat; bacteria enter via holes; cheese-wired dead fat feeds infection; result = staphylococcal pustules at entry points
  • Buried dermal sutures (simple interrupted, absorbable) preferred: no skin holes, no suture removal needed
  • Used routinely for carpal tunnel, trigger finger closure

Barrera: CTR postop: dressing off day 5, partial weight-bearing 5 lb, follow-up 10-14 days.

Closed Wound Management (Showering)

  • 30 years of evidence: early showering at 24 hours does NOT increase infection — may actually decrease it
  • Instructions: shower next day, hand elevated and quiet, water/mild soap OK, don’t rub wound, apply light clean dressing 3-4 days

Contaminated Wound Management

  • Sharply excise all dead tissue; irrigate copiously
  • WALANT in procedure room for most debridements — no delay for OR/sedation
  • Tap water = saline for irrigation (no difference in infection rates); tap water preferred for cost
  • Home wound care: daily shower rinse, clean dressing with thick layer of Vaseline
  • Vaseline keeps raw tissues alive (dermis, muscle, bone, tendon) by preventing desiccation; healing wound pushes out remaining Vaseline

Barrera: Skip Esmarch exsanguination for infection cases — go straight to tourniquet inflate. Contaminated tissue + milking pressure = proximal spread risk.

Exposed Raw Tissues

  • Old belief: exposed joint/tendon/bone always needs flap
  • Current: if tissues are alive AND kept moist (Vaseline or VAC), granulation tissue covers all exposed live tissues — skin graft or flap may not be required
  • Critical rule: never let raw live tissues dry, even for a few hours

Management Options for Large Open Finger Wounds

Option Advantages Disadvantages
Secondary healing Avoids further surgery, scarring, cold intolerance, numbness from flap dissection Prolonged healing (weeks), volar crease scar contracture
Skin graft (FTSG) Faster healing Donor scar, numb, less abrasion-resistant, may need later revision
Flap coverage Avoids contracture on flexor side, fastest return to healed wound Dissection damages skin/fat/nerve/vessels, additional scarring
  • Discuss all three options with patient when all are reasonable

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Clean vs contaminated is THE decision point — not time since injury
  • Buried absorbable sutures eliminate suture removal and reduce infection
  • Tap water irrigation is evidence-based and cost-effective
  • Vaseline is the key dressing adjunct for open wounds — prevents desiccation of exposed structures
  • Secondary healing with daily Vaseline + Coban can cover exposed bone/tendon without flap
  • Never leave raw tissue “open to air” — even a few hours causes desiccation necrosis
  • Patient reliability is essential for conservative wound management

Board-Style Questions (3-5)

  1. Q: A 45-year-old presents with a clean laceration over the dorsal hand sustained 36 hours ago. What is the most appropriate management? — A: Primary closure; clean wounds can be closed regardless of time since injury.

  2. Q: What is the advantage of buried absorbable dermal sutures over transcutaneous sutures for hand wound closure? — A: No skin puncture holes for bacterial entry; no suture removal required; lower risk of staphylococcal pustules.

  3. Q: A patient with a volar thumb soft tissue avulsion has exposed distal phalanx. What non-surgical management can achieve complete healing? — A: Daily shower, Vaseline, and Coban dressing; secondary healing with myofibroblast contraction covers exposed bone over ~10 weeks if tissues kept moist.

  4. Q: What irrigant is recommended for contaminated open hand wounds? — A: Tap water; no difference in infection rates compared to saline, and it is more practical/cost-effective.

  5. Q: What is the primary disadvantage of secondary healing for large volar finger wounds? — A: Scar band contracture at flexion creases inhibiting IP extension.
