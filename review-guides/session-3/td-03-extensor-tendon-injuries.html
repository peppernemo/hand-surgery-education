Extensor Tendon Injuries

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Extrinsic extensors form dorsal hood at MCP with sagittal bands to volar plate; centralize tendon + extend P1
  • Long extensor trifurcates distal to P1 → central slip (→ middle phalanx) + 2 lateral slips
  • Lateral bands (intrinsic + lateral slips) → terminal tendon → distal phalanx
  • Triangular ligament prevents dorsal subluxation of lateral bands; transverse retinacular ligament prevents dorsal migration at PIP
  • ORL: present < 50%; most often ulnar side ring finger
  • Prime MCP flexors = intrinsics (tendons volar to MCP axis, dorsal to PIP/DIP axes)
  • Grip strength markedly decreased with extensor paralysis
  • Quadriga concept: finger extensors function as single muscle unit — basis for relative motion splinting

  • Extensor digitorum brevis manus: aberrant muscle (PIN innervation); may be sole index extensor — do NOT excise

Thumb: EPL = primary central extensor; no lumbricals/interossei; APB (radial) + adductor (ulnar) → dorsal aponeurosis → IP extension

Classification

Finger Zones (odd = joint, even = bone):

Zone Location Key Structure
I DIP Terminal extensor
III PIP Central slip
V MCP Sagittal bands
VII Retinaculum Synovial sheath

Thumb: TI (IP) through TV (retinaculum)

Treatment

Zone I-II — Mallet Deformity

Doyle Classification: I = closed ± avulsion; II = open laceration; III = open + tissue loss; IVa = pediatric physeal; IVb = 20-50% articular; IVc = > 50% + volar subluxation

Closed (Type I): - DIP extension splint 24/7 × 6-8 weeks + gradual daytime weaning; leave PIP free - Even large bony fragments respond to splinting alone - Outcomes: 7.6° extensor lag, 12.8% complication rate

Bony mallet with DIP subluxation: - Extension block pinning (Ishiguro): 0.045 K-wire 45° to P2 head as block + second retrograde K-wire across DIP; remove 4-6 weeks

Open (Type II/III): 5-0 monofilament tendon repair + K-wire DIP pinning

Chronic mallet → swan neck: Fowler tenotomy (divide central extensor at PIP); wait ≥ 6 months; SORL reconstruction or FDS volar PIP tenodesis for fixed deformity

Zone III — Central Slip / Boutonniere

  • Initial presentation may be subtle — lateral bands can extend PIP early
  • Modified Elson test: PIP fully flexed against resistance; rigid DIP extension = positive (central slip injury)

Acute closed: PIP extension splint 6-8 weeks continuous + active DIP flexion hourly; then part-time 4-6 weeks

Acute open: < 50% → wound care + early motion; complete → ORIF with 4-0/5-0 + PIP pinning in extension × 5-6 weeks

Chronic boutonniere: - Boyes test: passive DIP flexion harder with PIP extended (vs pseudoboutonniere) - Conservative first — serial casting/splinting → relative motion flexion splint × 3 months - Surgical outcomes unpredictable; worse with PIP fixed flexion, age > 45, prior surgery - Curtis stepwise approach: TRL release → tenolysis → central slip reinforcement

Zone V — Sagittal Band Injuries

  • Closed sagittal band rupture: radial side middle/ring most common
  • Acute (< 3 wk): relative motion extension splint × 6 weeks — 95% success (Peelman)

  • 3 wk delay: only ~50% succeed with splinting → surgical repair

  • Fight bite: always evaluate for MCP joint penetration; aggressive I&D + IV antibiotics + delayed closure

Zone VI (Dorsum of Hand)

  • Thicker tendons; 3-0 core suture; > 80% good/excellent regardless of postop protocol
  • Loss of MCP flexion = most frequent complication

Zone VII-VIII

  • EPL rupture after non-displaced DRF → EIP-to-EPL transfer (optimal)

  • Zone VIII: don’t wait > 4 weeks — muscle contracture becomes permanent

  • Relative motion splinting is increasingly preferred for zones V-VI — leverages the quadriga concept, keeping the injured tendon in relative extension while permitting near-normal hand function and therapy

Complications & Outcomes

  • MCP extension contracture from prolonged splinting (most common)
  • Bunnell test (intrinsic tightness): MCP extended → passive PIP flexion difficult
  • vs Extrinsic tightness: MCP flexed → PIP flexion harder
  • Tenolysis: wait ≥ 4 months; therapy must be at plateau
  • Wong systematic review (zones IV-VII): TAM relative motion 205-236° vs dynamic 191-214° vs static 79-202°

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Odd zones = joints, even zones = bone segments — easy mnemonic
  • Modified Elson test is the best diagnostic for central slip injury
  • Mallet: splinting works even for large bony fragments without DIP subluxation
  • Sagittal band: splint within 3 weeks = 95% success; delay drops to ~50%
  • Fight bite at zone V — always explore for joint penetration; septic joint develops quickly
  • Extensor digitorum brevis manus is NOT pathology — do not excise
  • Bunnell vs extrinsic tightness test: opposite patterns of MCP-dependent PIP flexion

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A patient presents with a painful, stiff PIP joint after a dorsal laceration over the proximal phalanx. On exam, passive DIP flexion is more difficult with PIP extended than flexed. What does this indicate? — A: Boutonniere deformity (positive Boyes test) — lateral band dorsal subluxation tethers the terminal tendon with PIP extension.

  2. Q: A 30-year-old presents 2 weeks after a closed sagittal band rupture with extensor subluxation at the middle finger MCP. What is the best initial treatment? — A: Relative motion extension splint for 6 weeks — acute splinting within 3 weeks has a 95% success rate.

  3. Q: After non-displaced distal radius fracture, a patient develops inability to extend the thumb IP joint. What is the diagnosis and optimal treatment? — A: Attritional EPL rupture at Lister’s tubercle; EIP-to-EPL tendon transfer.

  4. Q: On exam, a patient has difficulty with passive PIP flexion when the MCP is extended, but PIP flexion improves with MCP flexed. Is this intrinsic or extrinsic tightness? — A: Intrinsic tightness (positive Bunnell test); extrinsic tightness shows the opposite pattern.

  5. Q: A bony mallet fracture involves 40% of the articular surface with no DIP subluxation. What is the recommended treatment? — A: DIP extension splint 24/7 for 6-8 weeks — even large bony fragments respond to splinting when the joint remains congruent.
