Wrist and Upper Limb Anomalies

Anatomy / Basic Science

Madelung’s Deformity

  • Premature physeal arrest of volar-ulnar distal radius; 4x more common in girls; clinically evident in adolescence
  • Vickers ligament: abnormal tether from lunate/TFCC to volar-ulnar radius metaphysis
  • Most commonly idiopathic; Madelung-like deformity from trauma, infection, Ollier, MHE, Turner
  • Leri-Weill dyschondrosteosis: autosomal dominant, SHOX gene haploinsufficiency → mesomelic dwarfism + Madelung’s

Proximal Radioulnar Synostosis

  • Failure of segmentation of radius and ulna; fixed in neutral to full pronation
  • Bilateral (asymmetric) in 60-80%; 1.5x more common in males

MHE (Multiple Hereditary Exostosis)

  • Autosomal dominant, 1:50,000; EXT1 or EXT2 loss-of-function mutations
  • EXT1 mutations more common and more severe phenotype
  • Forearm deformities in 30-60%: shortened ulna, radial bowing, ulnar wrist deviation, radial head dislocation
  • Malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma in up to 2% — cartilage cap > 2 cm on CT/MRI raises concern

Arthrogryposis (Amyoplasia)

  • 1 in 10,000; no definite genetic basis; normal intelligence
  • Characteristic position: shoulder IR, elbow extension, wrist flexion/UD, thumb-in-palm
  • Bilateral symmetric findings mandatory — asymmetry requires further investigation

Classification

Radioulnar Synostosis — Cleary and Omer

Type Description
I Radiocapitellar joint reduced, no bony synostosis
II Reduced, bony synostosis
III Bony synostosis + posterior radial head dislocation
IV Bony synostosis + anterior radial head dislocation

MHE Forearm — Jo (Modified Masada)

  • Type I: isolated distal ulna osteochondroma
  • Type II: distal ulna + radial head dislocation (IIA with, IIB without proximal radial tumor)
  • Type III: isolated distal radius osteochondroma
  • Type IV: both distal ulnar and radial (IVA no, IVB with radial head dislocation)

Workup

Madelung’s

  • Presentation: shortened forearm, volar sag, dorsally prominent ulna, ulnar-sided wrist pain
  • McCarroll diagnostic thresholds: ulnar tilt ≥ 33°, lunate subsidence ≥ 4 mm, lunate fossa angle ≥ 40°, palmar carpal displacement ≥ 20 mm
  • Whole forearm radiographs mandatory — some patients have radial head subluxation/dislocation

Treatment

Madelung’s

  • Observation: many asymptomatic patients with minimal deformity; 6-12 month XR follow-up
  • Physiolysis + Vickers ligament release: skeletally immature with growth remaining; volar approach, divide Vickers ligament, physiolysis with burr until normal cartilage, fat interposition
  • Dome osteotomy: most commonly used; 3D correction; volar approach, crescent osteotomy proximal to sigmoid notch, translate distal fragment dorsally/radially, K-wire fixation; lasting correction at 11 years
  • Ulnar shortening osteotomy: minimal radial deformity + prominent ulna; volar plate decreases hardware irritation

Radioulnar Synostosis

  • Most patients do NOT need surgery — shoulder and wrist compensate
  • Surgery for functional limitations (typically ≥ 60° fixed pronation)
  • Ezaki-Oishi technique: osteotomies of both radius (distal to PT insertion) and ulna (mid-distal 1/3); 1-2 cm segment removed, morsellized, replaced in intact periosteal sleeve; target neutral to slight pronation
  • Most feared complication: acute compartment syndrome — mitigate with fasciotomies, shortening, staged correction; admit overnight
  • Outcomes: average correction 74-78° with high satisfaction

MHE

  • Intervene for: pain, maintaining radiocapitellar joint, improving forearm rotation, aesthetics
  • Simple excision: painful tumors or those limiting rotation; substantial radial bow remodeling in young children
  • Ulnar tether release + osteochondroma excision: correct ulnar wrist deviation
  • Ulnar lengthening: for older patients with limited growth remaining
  • Radial head dislocation: reduction rarely indicated (recurs); symptomatic patients do well with radial head excision

Arthrogryposis — Key Procedures

  • Elbow release/tricepsplasty: W-plasty or VY-plasty in triceps + posterior capsulotomy → ≥ 90° flexion; 22/23 children able to self-feed (Van Heest)

  • Dorsal carpal wedge osteotomy: biplanar opening; ECU to ECRB transfer; goal neutral to +10° extension; 55° flexion → 11° at 5.7 years, 9.1/10 parent satisfaction (Foy)

  • Thumb release: Ezaki-Oishi flap, thenar release, MCP chondrodesis if unstable, EIP/EDQ to EPL transfer

  • Long head triceps transfer: requires good passive flexion ≥ 90° and adequate lateral head on MRI; fascia lata graft extension

  • DHO (distal humeral external rotation osteotomy): for cross-over grasp; ~90° external rotation; never combine with elbow release (severe flexion contracture risk)

  • Three adaptive hand-to-mouth mechanisms without active elbow flexion: table push, trunk thrust, cervical bend

  • Most kids with radioulnar synostosis adapt without surgery; operative candidates are typically those with ≥ 60° fixed pronation who can’t supinate enough for ADLs

  • Ezaki-Oishi technique creates a periosteum-tube pseudarthrosis with morsellized bone to prevent re-synostosis — overnight admission warranted (compartment syndrome is the high-stakes complication)

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Madelung’s: get whole forearm films — don’t miss proximal radial head pathology
  • Vickers ligament is the tether causing Madelung’s deformity — release it during physiolysis
  • SHOX haploinsufficiency → Leri-Weill → mesomelic dwarfism + Madelung’s
  • MHE: any osteochondroma with pain/rapid growth after skeletal maturity → rule out chondrosarcoma (cap > 2 cm)
  • Arthrogryposis: bilateral symmetry is mandatory — if asymmetric, reconsider diagnosis
  • Never combine DHO with elbow release in arthrogryposis (risk of severe flexion contracture)

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: What ligament must be released during surgical treatment of Madelung’s deformity? — A: The Vickers ligament (abnormal tether from lunate/TFCC to volar-ulnar radius metaphysis).
  2. Q: A child with bilateral symmetric elbow extension contractures, wrist flexion, and thumb-in-palm deformity is evaluated. What is the most likely diagnosis? — A: Arthrogryposis (amyoplasia) — bilateral symmetric positioning is characteristic.
  3. Q: A 14-year-old with MHE develops new pain at a forearm osteochondroma. MRI shows a 2.5-cm cartilage cap. What is the concern? — A: Malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma — cartilage cap > 2 cm after skeletal maturity is suspicious.
  4. Q: What is the most feared complication of derotation osteotomy for proximal radioulnar synostosis? — A: Acute compartment syndrome.
  5. Q: What genetic mutation causes Leri-Weill dyschondrosteosis and is associated with Madelung’s deformity? — A: SHOX gene haploinsufficiency (pseudoautosomal region of sex chromosomes).
