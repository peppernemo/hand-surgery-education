Scaphoid Fractures

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Most frequently fractured carpal bone; high nonunion risk
  • 3 reasons for nonunion: no cambium layer (no periosteal callus), flexion/humpback force, retrograde blood supply
  • Blood supply (Gelberman): 2 pedicles off radial artery
    • Distal pole pedicle → tubercle → supplies distal 20-30%
    • Dorsal ridge vessels → enter at/distal to waist → supply proximal 70-80%
    • No intraosseous anastomoses between the two
  • Proximal pole fracture nonunion rate 34% non-op (Eastley meta-analysis)
  • Distribution: waist 65%, distal pole 25%, proximal pole 10%

Classification

  • Mayo / Herbert / Russe — none reliably predict nonunion
  • Most useful in practice: location (proximal, waist, distal) + displacement (CT)
  • Unstable (Mayo): displacement > 1 mm, DISI, capitolunate > 15°, SL angle > 60°, lateral intrascaphoid > 35°, comminuted, perilunate
  • Arthroscopic probing > imaging for true instability (Buijze)

Workup

Exam

  • Snuffbox tenderness + scaphoid tubercle tenderness
  • Pain with ulnar deviation = only finding predictive of true fracture (ML study)

Imaging

  • Plain films: PA, lateral, scaphoid view (PA, ulnar deviation, 45° pronation)
  • Plain x-rays miss occult fractures — 13% missed even on follow-up
  • MRI ~100% sensitive for occult fracture; also assesses AVN (though AVN doesn’t predict union outcomes — Rancy)
  • CT = lower sensitivity, excellent specificity; best for displacement, location, comminution
  • Meta-analysis: immediate CT or MRI most cost-effective strategy

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Indications: tubercle, non/minimally displaced distal pole, non/minimally displaced waist fractures
  • Standard: short-arm cast, thumb-free × 10 wk (Buijze RCT) — long-arm and thumb-spica do not improve outcomes
  • Union 98% in CT-confirmed minimally displaced waist (Buijze)
  • Average time to union: distal 53d, waist 65d, proximal 113d (Grewal)
  • Check CT at 6-8 wk to confirm bone bridging — clinical exam unreliable since not all nonunions are painful

Operative

Indications

  • Displacement > 2 mm
  • Humpback deformity
  • All proximal pole fractures
  • Fracture-dislocations / concomitant DR fracture
  • Relative: comminution, delayed presentation (> 4 wk), patients needing faster RTW

Fixation

  • Single headless compression screw, central in scaphoid axis, as long as possible without joint penetration (Dodds)
  • Double-screw constructs only in high-demand athletes (↑ biomechanical strength but ↑ vascular + hardware risk)
  • Volar buttress plating for waist comminution / persistent humpback (frequent removal)

Approach by location

  • Proximal pole → dorsal (antegrade)
    • Mini-open: 1 cm distal + slight ulnar to Lister tubercle, transverse capsulotomy, wrist flexed, guidewire across fracture
    • Subtract 4-6 mm from measured length (avoid distraction, ensure subchondral seating)
  • Waist + distal pole → volar (retrograde)
    • Hockey-stick incision over FCR, identify + protect superficial radial artery branch
    • Resect portion of volar trapezium for proper screw start
    • DISI correction: temporary K-wire from radius into lunate at neutral, then extend wrist
    • K-wire derotation if needed
  • Arthroscopic-assisted (Geissler, Wong): allows direct visualization of reduction + assesses concomitant ligament injuries (Jørgsholm: high incidence)
  • Intraop fluoro views: AP, AP ulnar deviation, lateral, semipronated, semisupinated — all 5 to confirm screw centrality and avoid joint penetration

Post-op / Rehab

  • Short-arm thumb spica until CT shows > 50% bone bridging (typically 6-12 wk)
  • Athletes: cleared at > 50% bridging + restored ROM; average RTW 7.3 wk post-op (vs 13.9 wk post-cast removal in non-op) — systematic review: 98% return post-op vs 90% non-op
  • Cast immobilization during play not recommended (significant nonunion risk)

Complications & Outcomes

Complication Avoidance
Nonunion Right treatment for right fracture; smoking cessation
Humpback malunion Early fixation for at-risk fractures
Hardware prominence 5-view fluoro; common reason for reop
CRPS, nerve, infection Soft tissue handling, abx prophylaxis
  • SWIFFT RCT (Dias 2020): 408 waist fractures < 2 mm — no PRWE difference at 52 wk between surgery + cast; surgery faster at 12 wk but more complications (nerve, CRPS, infection)
  • Surgery: faster RTW, sometimes better grip/ROM (longer casts in non-op groups confound)
  • Heavy laborers: 7-week RTW benefit with surgery (Majeed)

Pediatric Pearls

  • Scaphoid = most common pediatric carpal fracture (0.45% of all peds fx); peak age ~15 yo
  • Ossifies last (~age 5 M / 4 F at distal pole, progresses proximally)
  • Waist 68% > distal 27% > proximal 6% in open physes; proximal % rises with maturity
  • 28-37% present as nonunions (athletes minimize symptoms)
  • MRI > CT (avoid pediatric ionizing radiation)
  • Acute nondisplaced: 90-99% heal in cast
  • Displaced waist/distal: 20-30% heal in cast → ORIF recommended
  • Proximal pole or small ossific nucleus → consider two 1.1 mm K-wires instead of screw to protect blood supply

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Snuffbox + tubercle tenderness with negative x-ray → straight to MRI (most cost-effective)
  • AVN on MRI does NOT predict union outcomes — don’t let it drive surgical decisions
  • Don’t trust radiographic “union” — CT for bone bridging
  • Central screw placement matters more than length
  • 5 fluoro views during ORIF, period
  • Many “unstable” Herbert B fractures are stable on arthroscopic probing

Board-Style Questions

  1. Q: What’s the nonunion rate for proximal pole scaphoid fractures treated non-operatively? A: 34% (Eastley meta-analysis) — operative fixation is standard.
  2. Q: Which fluoro views are required during scaphoid ORIF? A: AP, AP with ulnar deviation, lateral, semipronated, semisupinated.
  3. Q: Per the Buijze RCT, what is the optimal cast type for non-/minimally displaced waist fractures? A: Short-arm cast, thumb free, 10 weeks.
  4. Q: What does the SWIFFT RCT show about surgery vs cast for waist fractures < 2 mm displaced? A: No PRWE difference at 52 weeks; surgery faster at 12 weeks but more complications.
  5. Q: Why is the proximal pole at highest risk for AVN? A: Retrograde dorsal ridge vessel supply; no intraosseous anastomosis with distal pedicle.
