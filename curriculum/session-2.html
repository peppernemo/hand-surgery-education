Session 2 — Fractures & Dislocations

Week 3 of 8

Fractures of the upper extremity comprise a substantial portion of orthopaedic trauma. The distal radius is the most common long-bone fracture in adults and the most frequent operative case in any hand surgery practice. Scaphoid fractures are the most commonly missed carpal injury, with a high rate of nonunion when undiagnosed. Familiarity with classification systems, acceptable reduction parameters, and the indications for operative versus nonoperative management is essential.

Learning Objectives

  • Recite the acceptable reduction parameters for a distal radius fracture and justify the cutoff
  • Classify scaphoid fractures and explain why proximal-pole fractures are high-risk
  • Decide operative vs. non-operative for any metacarpal fracture based on location, angulation, and rotation
  • Recognize and manage a Bennett, Rolando, and reverse-Bennett fracture
  • Identify a Stener lesion and name the operative indications for thumb UCL injury
  • Work through a PIP fracture-dislocation and choose among closed, hemi-hamate, volar plate arthroplasty, and dynamic ex-fix

Questions

Anatomy & classification

  1. What are the acceptable reduction parameters for a distal radius fracture? (radial height, radial inclination, volar tilt, articular step)
  2. Name the Frykman classification axes — what two variables determine the number?
  3. Classify a scaphoid fracture by anatomic location and explain why location matters for healing.
  4. What is the Eaton-Littler volar plate classification of PIP fracture-dislocations, and how does the percentage of articular involvement guide treatment?
  5. What is the difference between a Bennett, Rolando, and reverse-Bennett fracture?

Exam maneuvers

  1. You see a patient 4 days out from a ground-level fall with a “normal” wrist x-ray, but he has anatomic snuffbox tenderness and pain with axial loading of the thumb. What’s your next step, and what’s the sensitivity/specificity of the initial x-ray for scaphoid fracture?
  2. A 24-year-old skier fell and now has pain at the base of the thumb. How do you examine for a UCL injury? What angle of laxity is diagnostic, and what’s a Stener lesion?

Clinical vignettes

  1. A 55-year-old woman falls onto an outstretched hand. X-rays show a dorsally angulated, intra-articular distal radius fracture with 3 mm of articular step-off and 25° of dorsal angulation. She is right-hand dominant and independently ambulatory. What’s your recommendation, and how did the WRIST trial change the thinking for patients > 60?
  2. A 19-year-old boxer punches a wall. Closed small finger metacarpal neck fracture with 45° of apex-dorsal angulation and no rotational deformity. Operate or splint? What’s your answer for the same fracture in the index metacarpal?
  3. A 20-year-old male with a thumb metacarpal base fracture. You see a small triangular volar-ulnar fragment remaining at the CMC joint and the rest of the metacarpal displaced radially and proximally. Name the fracture, the deforming forces, and the treatment.
  4. A 35-year-old woman closes her hand in a car door, sustains a dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation of the long finger with 45% articular involvement of the volar middle phalanx base. What are your three surgical options and how do you choose?
  5. A 45-year-old man with a “jammed” finger now has pain at the thumb MCP 3 weeks later. Stress testing shows 40° of radial deviation with a firm endpoint compared to 15° on the contralateral side. Ultrasound suggests a displaced UCL. What does the history tell you, what’s your diagnosis, and what’s the treatment?

Answer Key

  1. Acceptable reduction (distal radius):

    • Radial height: within 2-3 mm of contralateral (~11 mm normal)
    • Radial inclination: ≥ 15° (normal ~22°)
    • Volar tilt: neutral to 10° dorsal acceptable (normal 11° volar); any dorsal angulation > 10° is concerning, especially in younger patients
    • Articular step-off: ≤ 2 mm (some accept ≤ 1 mm for younger/active)
    • DRUJ congruent

  2. Frykman uses two axes: intra-articular vs extra-articular (radiocarpal +/- DRUJ), and ulnar styloid involvement (yes/no). Eight types total, evens = ulnar styloid fx, intra-articular rising with number.

  3. Scaphoid anatomic location:

    • Proximal pole (~10%): worst blood supply (retrograde flow via dorsal ridge branches of radial artery); highest nonunion rate
    • Waist (~70%): most common; moderate healing
    • Distal pole / tubercle (~20%): best blood supply, usually heals with casting
    • Blood supply: dorsal carpal branch of radial artery enters the dorsal ridge and flows proximally — proximal pole is end-vascular.

  4. Eaton-Littler (PIP fracture-dislocation):

    • <30% articular involvement: stable → extension block splint, early motion
    • 30-50%: tenuous → often surgical (hemi-hamate or dynamic ex-fix)
    • >50%: unstable → hemi-hamate autograft or volar plate arthroplasty; dynamic ex-fix for comminution

  5. Thumb metacarpal base fractures:

    • Bennett: intra-articular 2-part fracture of thumb metacarpal base; volar-ulnar “beak” fragment stays (anchored by anterior oblique ligament), metacarpal shaft pulled radially/proximally by APL and adductor pollicis
    • Rolando: intra-articular comminuted (Y- or T-shaped) — worse prognosis
    • Reverse-Bennett: intra-articular fracture of 5th metacarpal base, hook of hamate fragment small and the shaft pulled ulnarly/proximally by ECU

  6. Occult scaphoid fracture. Initial x-ray sensitivity ~70-80%. Options: thumb spica splint and repeat x-rays in 10-14 days, OR immediate MRI (~100% sensitivity) or CT if high suspicion or the patient cannot tolerate empiric immobilization (manual laborer, athlete). Low threshold for MRI — missed scaphoid = nonunion → SNAC wrist.

  7. Thumb UCL exam. Stress the MCP in full extension (tests proper UCL) and 30° flexion (tests accessory UCL). >30° laxity or >15° side-to-side difference = complete tear. Stener lesion = the adductor aponeurosis becomes interposed between the torn UCL and its insertion on the proximal phalanx, preventing healing → surgical indication.

  8. Intra-articular DRF. Step-off > 2 mm + dorsal angulation 25° in an active, independent patient → ORIF (volar locking plate standard). WRIST trial (Chung et al): in patients ≥ 60, non-operative treatment had equivalent 1-year patient-rated outcomes compared with volar plating, ex-fix, or K-wires — so for a truly sedentary elderly patient, non-op is defensible. But “independently ambulatory, right-hand dominant” argues for surgery regardless of age.

  9. Metacarpal neck angulation tolerances:

    • Small finger: up to 40-50° acceptable (compensated by CMC mobility)
    • Ring: up to 30-40°
    • Long: up to 15-20°
    • Index: up to 10-15° (stiff CMC — no compensatory motion)
    • Any rotational deformity = surgical indication. So: 45° boxer’s fracture of the small MC with no rotation → splint / brief immobilization → early motion. Same fracture in the index → operate.

  10. Bennett fracture. Deforming forces: APL pulls the shaft radially and proximally; adductor pollicis adducts and supinates; the volar-ulnar beak fragment is anchored by the anterior oblique ligament and stays reduced. Treatment: closed reduction + percutaneous pinning (K-wires, may not engage the fragment itself if small) or ORIF if fragment is > 20-30% of articular surface.

  11. Dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation, 45% articular involvement.

    • Closed reduction + extension block splinting: only if <30-40%, stable through full flexion
    • Hemi-hamate autograft (Heyworth/Hastings): replaces the comminuted/absent volar lip with a hemi-hamate graft — best for acute injuries 30-60% with viable articular cartilage dorsally
    • Volar plate arthroplasty (Eaton): interpose the volar plate into the defect — older indication, limited motion
    • Dynamic ex-fix (Suzuki / Agee): distraction ligamentotaxis — best for very comminuted “pilon” injuries For 45% with a single fragment: hemi-hamate is the modern choice.

  12. Chronic thumb UCL injury with Stener lesion. “Jammed” with instability now confirms a UCL tear rather than simple sprain. Stress > 30° with firm (adductor aponeurosis) endpoint + imaging = Stener. Treatment: open repair if acute (< 4-6 weeks) with suture anchor; reconstruction (FCR or palmaris graft) if chronic > 6 weeks.

  13. b. Lower rate of nonunion and malunion. 2 mm of displacement = unstable scaphoid fracture; nonunion rate rises dramatically with displacement. Cosmesis and patient satisfaction are not the indication.

  14. b. Radial shaft fracture with DRUJ disruption. (Monteggia = ulnar shaft + radial head dislocation. Essex-Lopresti = radial head fx + DRUJ dislocation + IOM disruption.)

  15. Complications after distal radius ORIF:

    • EPL rupture: late, attritional — fraying against Lister’s tubercle or a prominent dorsal screw. Warning: inability to retropulse thumb off table. Treatment: EIP to EPL transfer.
    • Flexor tendon irritation / rupture (FPL most common): prominent volar screws past volar rim or plate too distal (beyond watershed line). Soong grade. Warning: pain with thumb flexion, FPL weakness, crepitus. Remove plate, tendon reconstruction if ruptured.
    • Median neuropathy: acute carpal tunnel after DRF (rapid, pressure-related) → emergent release. Late / chronic median symptoms → routine CTR if persistent.
    • Stiffness: early hand therapy is the intervention. Finger ROM day 1 post-op.
    • CRPS: vigilance in the first 6 weeks; referral to pain/OT, vitamin C prophylaxis (500 mg daily × 50 days) has modest evidence.
