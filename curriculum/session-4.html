Session 4 — Compressive Neuropathies & Common Electives

Week 7 of 8

Compressive neuropathies — most prominently carpal tunnel syndrome — represent the highest-volume operative work in any hand surgery practice. Together with the common elective conditions of trigger finger, De Quervain’s tenosynovitis, Dupuytren’s disease, and thumb basal joint arthritis, these constitute the bulk of clinic visits and elective surgical scheduling. A working knowledge of anatomy, provocative examination, and evidence-based surgical indications is essential.

Learning Objectives

  • Work through a CTS patient: history, CTS-6, exam, when to order EDX, surgical technique choice
  • Differentiate CTS, pronator, AIN, cubital tunnel, Guyon’s, radial tunnel, and PIN by symptom pattern and exam finding
  • Grade cubital tunnel severity (McGowan/Dellon) and state the decision point for in situ release vs transposition
  • Classify trigger finger (Quinnell / Green), describe the steroid injection technique, and operate on a failed case
  • Walk through the Dupuytren’s treatment menu: collagenase, needle aponeurotomy, fasciectomy — who gets what
  • Stage thumb CMC arthritis (Eaton-Littler) and choose among splint/injection, trapeziectomy ± LRTI, and suspensionplasty

Questions

Anatomy & classification

  1. List the 9 structures that pass through the carpal tunnel and name the one whose compression causes CTS symptoms in the radial 3.5 digits.
  2. Walk through the CTS-6 score — the 6 items, their weights, the cutoff, and what the score means for needing EDX.
  3. Five anatomic sites of potential ulnar nerve compression at the elbow (cubital tunnel). Name them in order from proximal to distal.
  4. Grade cubital tunnel severity (McGowan + Dellon). What’s the clinical threshold for operative treatment, and for transposition vs in situ release?
  5. Name the Eaton-Littler stages of thumb CMC arthritis and state the dominant treatment for each stage.
  6. Name the cords of Dupuytren’s disease anatomically (pretendinous, spiral, lateral, central, retrovascular) and identify which cord displaces the neurovascular bundle centrally and superficially.

Exam maneuvers

  1. Describe Durkan’s, Phalen’s, and Tinel’s at the wrist — specific technique, duration, and reported sensitivity/specificity for CTS.
  2. How do you exam-test for AIN palsy vs high median nerve injury?
  3. How do you differentiate De Quervain’s tenosynovitis from thumb CMC arthritis on history and exam?

Clinical vignettes

  1. A 55-year-old woman with nocturnal numbness in the thumb/index/long for 1 year. CTS-6 is 18. Exam: Durkan’s positive, Phalen’s positive, mild thenar atrophy, 2PD 6 mm thumb. Do you need EDX? What’s her operative plan — open CTR, endoscopic, or either?
  2. A 60-year-old diabetic with progressive ring and small finger numbness, weakness gripping, hypothenar and intrinsic atrophy. Exam: Tinel at cubital tunnel, Froment’s positive, Wartenberg’s sign positive. McGowan grade? Operation choice and why?
  3. A 50-year-old with A1 pulley tenderness and a palpable trigger of the long finger that locks in flexion and requires passive extension to release. Green grade? First-line treatment. If that fails, what next and how many injections before operating?
  4. A 65-year-old man with a palpable cord in the palm extending to the ring finger PIP with 45° MCP flexion contracture and 30° PIP flexion contracture. Tabletop test positive. What are his three treatment options, the evidence for each, and your recommendation?
  5. A 62-year-old woman with 2 years of thumb base pain. Grind test positive, painful weak pinch, x-ray shows narrowed CMC joint, subchondral sclerosis, and small osteophytes; STT joint is preserved. Stage? First-line treatment, and when would you operate?

Answer Key

See ~/JanosClaude/templates/compressive-neuropathies-handout.md for detailed references. Summary answers:

  1. Carpal tunnel contents: 4 FDS + 4 FDP + 1 FPL + median nerve. Median nerve compression causes the radial-3.5-digit symptoms; palmar cutaneous branch travels over the TCL so it’s spared (palmar thumb base sensation intact in classic CTS — helpful differentiator from a high median nerve lesion).

  2. CTS-6 (Graham et al., 2006; Fowler et al., 2023): | Item | Points | |—|—| | Numbness in median nerve territory | 3.5 | | Nocturnal numbness | 4.0 | | Thenar weakness / atrophy | 5.0 | | Positive Phalen’s | 5.0 | | Loss of 2PD ≤ 5 mm | 4.5 | | Positive Tinel’s at wrist | 4.0 |

    • Max 26. Cutoff > 12 = ~80% probability of CTS → EDX may not be needed if clinical picture is consistent. ≤ 12 → get EDX.

  3. Cubital tunnel — 5 sites of compression (proximal → distal):

    1. Arcade of Struthers (~8 cm proximal to medial epicondyle)
    2. Medial intermuscular septum
    3. Medial epicondyle (retrocondylar groove)
    4. Osborne’s ligament (between the two heads of FCU)
    5. Deep FCU fascia (distal edge)

  4. Cubital tunnel grading:

    • McGowan I: subjective symptoms only (paresthesias) — non-op, activity modification, night splinting, NSAIDs
    • McGowan II: weakness of intrinsics without atrophy — surgery (in situ decompression vs transposition)
    • McGowan III: intrinsic atrophy, measurable motor deficit — surgery
    • Dellon adds a “severity” component with finer grading of sensory/motor
    • In situ vs transposition: modern data (ASSH CPG, randomized trials) show in situ decompression is equivalent to transposition for primary idiopathic cubital tunnel in most cases. Transposition (subcutaneous or submuscular) reserved for subluxing nerve, revision, or clear structural compression distal to the cubital tunnel.

  5. Eaton-Littler thumb CMC staging:

    • Stage I: joint space widening, < 1/3 subluxation — splint/CSI, thumb CMC orthosis
    • Stage II: narrowing, osteophytes/loose bodies < 2 mm — splint/CSI; surgery if symptomatic
    • Stage III: joint space obliterated, osteophytes/loose bodies > 2 mm, STT joint spared — trapeziectomy ± LRTI or suspensionplasty
    • Stage IV: pantrapezial arthritis (STT also involved) — trapeziectomy + STT treatment or fusion

  6. Dupuytren’s cords: pretendinous (palm), central (palm and digit), spiral (palm and digit), lateral (digit), retrovascular (digit). Spiral cord displaces the neurovascular bundle centrally, volarly, and proximally — this is why digital nerves are at risk during fasciectomy, especially at the PIP level.

  7. Provocative maneuvers (CTS):

    • Durkan’s: direct pressure over the carpal tunnel for 30 sec — sens ~87%, spec ~90% (most sensitive)
    • Phalen’s: wrist flexion 60 sec — sens ~68%
    • Tinel’s at the wrist: tapping over the median nerve — sens ~50% (most specific when positive but low sens)

  8. AIN vs high median:

    • AIN palsy: loss of FPL (thumb IP flexion), FDP to index (DIP flexion), pronator quadratus. Inability to make an “OK” sign — thumb IP stays extended, index DIP stays extended. No sensory loss.
    • High median (at elbow): loss of FPL, FDP to index/long, FDS to all, FCR, PT, pronator quadratus, plus median sensory loss in the radial 3.5 digits. Sensory loss is the distinguisher.

  9. De Quervain’s vs thumb CMC:

    • De Quervain’s: pain over the first dorsal compartment (APL + EPB) at the radial styloid. Finkelstein / Eichhoff test positive. Usually women 30-50, post-partum common. Tenderness is dorsoradial, not at the CMC.
    • Thumb CMC: pain at the thumb base with pinch. Grind test positive (axial compression + rotation at the CMC). Tenderness is at the CMC joint, weakness with pinch/grip, x-ray changes. Older patient.

  10. Clinical CTS, CTS-6 = 18. Score > 12 with consistent clinical picture → EDX is not required (Graham/Fowler evidence). Offer surgical release. Open vs endoscopic: equivalent long-term outcomes in multiple RCTs; endoscopic has earlier return to work by ~1-2 weeks and slightly less scar tenderness early, but a higher rate of transient neurapraxia and recurrence in some series. For a primary idiopathic case with no unusual anatomy, either is appropriate — operator preference and patient factors drive the choice.

  11. Cubital tunnel, McGowan III (atrophy + motor deficit). Operate. In situ simple decompression is the modern default unless there is nerve subluxation, revision, or distal compression (past the cubital tunnel). Consider anterior transposition (subcutaneous or submuscular) for subluxing nerve or revision. Medial epicondylectomy is a legacy option — less commonly used.

  12. Trigger finger, Green/Quinnell grade:

  • Green: I pretriggering (pain), II active trigger, III passive trigger (requires other hand to unlock), IV fixed flexion contracture
  • This patient is Grade III. First-line: corticosteroid injection into the flexor sheath (80% efficacy first injection, ~50% second). Up to 2 injections reasonable; operate (A1 pulley release, open or percutaneous) after failed second injection, any grade IV, and relative indication for single injection in diabetics (lower efficacy of steroids, higher progression rate).

  1. Dupuytren’s with MCP + PIP contracture. Tabletop positive = functional impairment, operative indication. Options:

    • Collagenase (CORD I, CORD II): injection into cord, manipulation 24-72 h later. Works well for MCP cords (MCP recovers easily); less effective for PIP cords. ~60-80% reduction to < 5° of contracture at MCP; recurrence ~47% at 5 yrs.
    • Needle aponeurotomy: percutaneous transection with a needle. Minimal recovery time. Good for MCP cords, less effective for PIP, and higher recurrence than fasciectomy.
    • Open fasciectomy (limited, not radical): gold standard for severe/recurrent disease and PIP involvement. Lowest recurrence (~20-30% at 5 yrs) but longer recovery, wound complications, and nerve/artery risk (spiral cord). For this patient: open limited fasciectomy is the strongest choice given PIP involvement. Discuss collagenase as less invasive but with higher PIP recurrence.

  2. Thumb CMC arthritis, Eaton III (joint space obliterated, STT spared). First-line: CMC orthosis (long or short opponens), activity modification, CSI, NSAIDs. Operate when non-op fails and the patient has functional limitation: trapeziectomy ± LRTI (ligament reconstruction + tendon interposition with FCR) is workhorse; suspensionplasty (suture-button, Mini TightRope) is an alternative with faster recovery and similar outcomes in emerging literature. Arthrodesis and prosthetic arthroplasty are less commonly used at this center.

  3. c. Durkan’s (carpal compression test) — sens ~87%, most sensitive of the physical exam maneuvers.

  4. b. Palmar cutaneous branch of median nerve. Arises 4-6 cm proximal to the wrist crease, travels between FCR and PL, and can be injured by an incision placed radial to the standard palmar (ulnar to thenar crease) incision. A scar neuroma here causes painful scar syndrome and is the most common iatrogenic injury in CTR.
