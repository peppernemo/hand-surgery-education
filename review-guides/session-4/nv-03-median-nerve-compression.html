Median Nerve Compression

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Median nerve: C5-T1, formed by medial + lateral cords
  • Crosses anterior to brachial artery at mid-humerus (lateral→medial)
  • Ligament of Struthers: fibrous band from supracondylar process to medial epicondyle (1-4% of population); can compress median nerve ± brachial artery
  • Compression sites (proximal → distal): ligament of Struthers → lacertus fibrosus → pronator teres (between heads) → FDS arch → carpal tunnel
  • Martin-Gruber anastomosis (forearm median→ulnar motor crossover): 23% prevalence; bilateral in 19%; Type I (60%) and Type II (35%) most common
  • AIN: largest muscular branch; arises 5-8 cm distal to medial epicondyle; innervates FPL, FDP (index/long), PQ; terminal sensory branch to volar wrist joint
  • Gantzer’s muscle (accessory FPL head): present in up to 68% — a normal variant, not rare
  • Carpal tunnel boundaries: carpal bones dorsally, TCL palmarly; narrowest at hook of hamate (20x10 mm)
  • TCL: attaches pisiform/hamate (ulnar) to scaphoid/trapezium (radial); thickest at middle-distal third junction (up to 6 mm in CTS); division reduces carpal arch stiffness only 7%
  • Recurrent motor branch: extraligamentous 46%, subligamentous 31%, transligamentous 23% (Lanz)
  • Bifid median nerve: 2.8% prevalence; associated with persistent median artery in 79%

Workup

History

  • Pronator syndrome: vague forearm ache, daytime symptoms with pronation/supination, numbness in palmar cutaneous distribution (thenar eminence — distinguishes from CTS)
  • AIN syndrome (Kiloh-Nevin): sudden forearm pain → weakness/loss of FPL + index FDP + PQ; no sensory loss; overlap with Parsonage-Turner syndrome
  • CTS: nocturnal paresthesias (hallmark), bimodal peaks (5th + 8th decades), female:male 3:1; > 500,000 releases/year in US

Exam

  • Pronator syndrome: tenderness over proximal PT, positive pronator compression test (30 sec), no Tinel at wrist, no Phalen/Durkan; resisted supination → bicipital aponeurosis; resisted long finger PIP flexion → FDS arch
  • AIN syndrome: Kiloh-Nevin sign (cannot make “OK” sign — compensatory FPB/FDS flexion instead)
  • CTS provocative tests: Phalen (sens 68%, spec 73%), Tinel (50%, 77%), Durkan (64%, 83%), combined Durkan+Phalen (80%, 92%)
  • CTS-6: numbness in median distribution, nocturnal numbness, thenar weakness/atrophy, Tinel, Phalen, loss of 2PD — probability score correlates with CTS diagnosis

Barrera: CTS-6 score > 12 = positive for CTS.

  • Sensory testing pearl: Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments test threshold (earlier finding); static 2PD tests innervation density (late finding) — use both for every suspected compression neuropathy

Electrodiagnostics

  • CTS: prolonged median sensory peak latency > 4.0 ms at wrist; motor distal latency > 4.5 ms; median-ulnar difference > 0.5 ms (sensory) or > 1.5 ms (motor)
  • Severity: mild (sensory only), moderate (sensory + motor), severe (absent sensory + abnormal motor), extreme (both absent)
  • Pronator syndrome: often normal — NCS helps rule out other sites; normal wrist conduction + clinical findings → supports proximal compression
  • AIN syndrome: acute denervation limited to AIN-innervated muscles; 60% have concomitant abnormalities in non-AIN distributions

Treatment

Pronator Syndrome

  • Non-operative: activity modification, nerve glides, stretching, brief immobilization; Morris: 5/7 responded to intramuscular PT steroid injection
  • Operative (> 3 months persistent symptoms): lazy-S incision from 5 cm above medial epicondyle across antecubital fossa extending distally 10 cm; release all compressive structures (lacertus, PT heads, FDS arch, ligament of Struthers if present); protect MABC nerves, median motor branches, radial artery
  • Post-op: splint 7-10 days (elbow 90°, forearm 45° pronation, wrist slight flexion); ROM at 7-10 days; resistive exercises at 6-8 weeks
  • Outcomes: complete relief 22%, improvement 63%, unchanged 9% (Hartz); minimally invasive approaches achieving 93% resolution (Zancolli)

AIN Syndrome

  • Non-operative first: rest, immobilization, avoid provocative activities; onset of recovery 5.9 mo (range 3-12); full recovery 9.5 mo; age < 40 = better prognosis
  • Operative (6 months without recovery + confirmed EDX + no other nerve lesions): same approach as pronator; consider interfascicular neurolysis for hourglass constrictions (Sneag: fascicular constrictions proximal to elbow in all 22 patients on MR neurography)

CTS — Non-operative

  • Wrist neutral splint (especially nocturnal); neutral > 20° extension
  • Steroid injection: benefits up to 6 months; 32% never need further treatment (Evers, 7.4 yr follow-up); diabetics 2.6x more likely to recur within 1 year

Barrera: CSI = Kenalog 10 mg/mL + 1% lido (never Depo).

  • CTS injection technique: enter ulnar to palmaris longus, just proximal to the distal wrist crease, angling 30-45 degrees

CTS — Operative

  • Indications: thenar atrophy, constant numbness, severe EDX changes, symptoms > 1 year, failed 6-week non-operative trial
  • Open mini CTR (authors’ preferred): 3-cm incision from distal wrist crease in ring finger axis; divide TCL distal→proximal with 2-mm cuff on hook of hamate; release antebrachial fascia 2-3 cm proximal; inspect for anomalies; no routine tenosynovectomy or neurolysis
  • Endoscopic (Agee single-portal): 1-cm incision at wrist; divide TCL under direct visualization; faster return to work; higher nerve injury risk (mostly transient)
  • No beneficial effect of post-op immobilization demonstrated (multiple studies)
  • Outcomes: > 95% good/excellent; endoscopic preferred by 66% of patients (scar/pillar pain); revision rate 1-5%

Revision CTS

  • Persistent (no relief) vs recurrent (relief then return after 6 mo) vs new symptoms (iatrogenic)
  • Extended open approach; identify nerve proximal to scar in native tissue; external neurolysis
  • Hypothenar fat pad flap (Strickland): well-vascularized coverage over scarred median nerve; ulnar-artery-based perforators

Complications & Outcomes

  • Pronator syndrome: incomplete decompression up to 55%; MABC neuroma risk
  • AIN: incomplete correction most common; Park: 10/11 achieved M4 within 12 mo post-surgery
  • CTS: pillar pain (12.7%), burning discomfort (18%), scar tenderness (7.3%); structural nerve/vessel/tendon injury rare with either approach
  • Revision CTS: challenging; 41-91% persistent symptoms after revision; recurrent group improves most

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Thenar eminence numbness = pronator syndrome (palmar cutaneous branch doesn’t traverse carpal tunnel)
  • AIN syndrome and Parsonage-Turner overlap significantly — EDX + MRI help distinguish; PT has broader nerve involvement + severe initial pain
  • CTS + pronator syndrome can coexist (double crush) — failed CTR should prompt proximal evaluation
  • Incision for open CTR: ring finger axis, 5+ mm ulnar to interthenar depression to protect palmar cutaneous branch
  • Kaplan’s cardinal line (parallel to abducted thumb ulnar border → hook of hamate) = distal TCL extent
  • Recurrent motor branch exits 6.5 mm from distal TCL edge in ring finger axis — respect the fat pad

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A patient with forearm aching and numbness over the thenar eminence has a negative Phalen and Tinel at the wrist. What is the most likely diagnosis? — A: Pronator syndrome; palmar cutaneous branch involvement distinguishes it from CTS, and negative wrist provocative tests support proximal compression.
  2. Q: A 35-year-old presents with inability to make an “OK” sign after sudden severe shoulder pain 2 weeks ago. EMG shows denervation in FPL, FDP index, and brachioradialis. What is the diagnosis? — A: Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy); brachioradialis involvement extends beyond isolated AIN territory, and the presentation with severe initial pain is classic.
  3. Q: What are the three variations of the recurrent motor branch course relative to the TCL? — A: Extraligamentous (46%), subligamentous (31%), transligamentous (23%) per the Lanz classification.
  4. Q: What is the most reliable MRI finding in AIN syndrome? — A: Edema within the pronator quadratus on T2-weighted fat-saturated images (Dunn); fascicular constrictions proximal to the elbow on MR neurography (Sneag).
  5. Q: After open carpal tunnel release, a patient reports persistent numbness. At revision, what is the most common finding? — A: Incomplete TCL release; also dense scar tissue with median nerve adherent to radial TCL leaflet. MABC neuroma found in 73% of failed cubital tunnels (analogous risk in CTR with cutaneous nerve injury).
