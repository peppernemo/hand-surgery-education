Vascular Disorders

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Superficial palmar arch complete in 81.3%; deep palmar arch complete in 95.2%
  • Only 10-20% of normal hand blood flow for metabolic demands; 80-90% is thermoregulatory
  • Endothelial regulation: endothelin (vasoconstriction) vs EDRF (vasodilation)
  • Classification by etiology: embolic, thrombotic, systemic, iatrogenic
  • Classification by arterial layer (Fuchs): endothelium → thromboemboli; intima → atherosclerosis; media → aneurysm; adventitia → arteritis

Workup

History

  • Pain, paresthesias, cold intolerance, color changes with cold/stress
  • Digital ulcerations, necrosis, prior amputations
  • Rheumatologic conditions, hypercoagulability, smoking

Exam

  • Pallor, cyanosis, mottling, subungual hemorrhages, clubbing
  • Brachial, radial, ulnar pulses
  • Allen’s test
  • Hand-held Doppler: palmar arch, dorsal radial artery, proper digital vessels
  • Cold water provocation for Raynaud’s

Imaging

  • Conventional angiography = gold standard (best detail; can use intra-arterial vasodilators)
  • Digital brachial index (DBI): normal 0.9; ≤ 0.7 = inadequate perfusion
  • Doppler US for flow + thrombus + aneurysm characterization
  • CTA/MRA for larger vessels; insufficient for digital arteries
  • Echo for embolic source

Hypothenar Hammer Syndrome (HHS)

Key Facts

  • Most common occlusive disease of the hand
  • Ulnar artery aneurysm + thrombosis at hook of hamate
  • Incidence 1.6-14% in manual laborers, craftsmen, athletes
  • Mechanism: ulnar artery superficial + tethered by dorsal branch → repetitive trauma → media damage → aneurysmal dilation → thrombosis → distal embolization
  • Angiography: pathognomonic “corkscrew” tortuous pattern with stenotic + ectatic segments

Non-operative

  • Activity modification, smoking cessation
  • CCBs, alpha-blockers, aspirin/clopidogrel
  • Stellate ganglion / brachial plexus block for acute critical ischemia
  • Intra-arterial thrombolytics (bleeding complications up to 10%)

Operative

  • Indications: reconstructible lesion, acute ischemia, chronic ischemia with threatened tissue, failed medical management
  • Resect diseased segment until normal tunica intima + media
  • Reversed vein graft (volar forearm or saphenous) — most common reconstruction
  • May need branched graft to reconstruct common digital vessels
  • 8-0 nylon microsuture; end-to-side of common digitals into graft if needed

Post-op

  • Wrist splint × 2 wk; fingers free
  • Heparin × 3-5 d; ASA × 1 mo

Outcomes — The HHS Paradox

  • 78% of grafts occlude by 10 yr (Endress) — yet patients remain satisfied
  • Even with occlusion, temporary flow restoration allows tissue healing + collateral development → sustained symptom improvement
  • Patent grafts = significantly less cold sensitivity
  • Vein grafts outperform arterial grafts for patency

Raynaud’s Phenomenon

Primary (Raynaud’s Disease)

  • Vasospasm without tissue injury
  • Bilateral symmetric, no necrosis, normal inflammatory labs, ANA negative

Secondary

  • Underlying systemic disease (SSc, SLE, MCTD)
  • Progressive ischemia, ulcerations
  • > 80% of SSc patients have Raynaud’s at diagnosis; > 40% develop digital ulcers

Treatment — Non-operative

  • Lifestyle modification, cold avoidance, wound care, smoking cessation
  • Nifedipine (CCB) = first-line pharmacotherapy
  • PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil)
  • ACE inhibitors
  • Botulinum toxin: 50 U/hand distributed across common digital and proper digital arteries; RCT shows improved QuickDASH + pain

Treatment — Operative

  • Periarterial sympathectomy (adventitial stripping 1.5-2 cm)

    • 4 incisions: radial artery at wrist, ulnar artery at wrist, superficial arch + 3 common digitals (transverse mid-palm), radial artery in snuffbox
    • Circumferential adventitia stripping; separate from venae comitantes; divide nerve branches

  • Post-op: compressive dressing × 3-5 d; immediate digital ROM

  • Counsel patients that recurrence after sympathectomy is common; a substantial percentage still ultimately need amputation (Hartzell: 26% required amputation at 90 mo)

  • Earlier referral to surgery may be better than waiting for tissue loss (Momeni: advocates earlier intervention)

Sympathectomy Outcomes

  • Ruch (22 pts, 46 mo): 82% decreased pain; 90% avoided amputation
  • Hartzell (90 mo): 75% fewer ulcers or complete healing; 26% still required amputation
  • Momeni: 92.3% pain improvement — advocates earlier surgical intervention

CCB Outcomes (Cochrane 2017)

  • 38 RCTs, 982 patients: reduce frequency, duration, severity, pain, disability
  • Greater effect in primary Raynaud’s

End-Stage Ischemia — Venous Arterialization

  • For failed revascularization or no distal arterial targets
  • Reverse flow through veins delivers oxygenated blood
  • Cephalic vein most common target; donor = dorsal radial, radial, or ulnar artery
  • Selective side-branch ligation + valvulotomy forces flow distally
  • Allows healing + collateral development; doesn’t preclude future AV fistula
  • Long-term outcomes unknown

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • HHS is the most common hand occlusive disease — ask about occupational/sports hand-hammering
  • “Corkscrew” on angiography = pathognomonic for HHS
  • HHS grafts often occlude but patients still improve — don’t withhold surgery
  • Primary Raynaud’s = bilateral, symmetric, ANA-negative, no necrosis
  • Nifedipine first-line for Raynaud’s; botox emerging option
  • Conventional angiography remains gold standard — CTA/MRA insufficient for digital arteries
  • DBI ≤ 0.7 = inadequate perfusion

Board-Style Questions

  1. Q: What is the most common occlusive vascular disease of the hand? A: Hypothenar hammer syndrome — ulnar artery aneurysm/thrombosis at the hook of hamate.
  2. Q: What is the pathognomonic angiographic finding in HHS? A: “Corkscrew” tortuous appearance with alternating stenotic and ectatic segments.
  3. Q: What percentage of patients with systemic sclerosis develop digital ulcers? A: >40% (large multinational study).
  4. Q: What criteria distinguish primary from secondary Raynaud’s? A: Primary: bilateral symmetric, no necrosis, normal inflammatory labs, negative ANA.
  5. Q: What is the first-line pharmacotherapy for Raynaud’s phenomenon? A: Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine).
