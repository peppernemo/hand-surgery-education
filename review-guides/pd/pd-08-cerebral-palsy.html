Cerebral Palsy — Upper Extremity

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Nonprogressive disturbance of the developing fetal brain → motor disorder
  • Upper motor neuron injury → spasticity of agonists + weakness of antagonists
  • Primary problem (CNS) → secondary (peripheral muscle imbalance, joint malpositioning) → tertiary (fixed deformities, contractures)
  • Primary problem generally not remediable; secondary/tertiary are targets for intervention

Typical UE Posture

Joint Spastic (overpull) Weak
Elbow Biceps, brachialis → flexion Triceps
Forearm PT, PQ → pronation Supinator
Wrist FCU, FCR → flexion/UD ECRL/B, ECU
Fingers FDS, FDP → clenched fist EDC, EIP/EDQ
Thumb Adductor, FPB → in-palm EPL, EPB, APL

Distribution Patterns

  • Monoplegia, hemiplegia, diplegia, triplegia, quadriplegia/tetraplegia

Workup

Evaluation

  • Hand dominance before 18-24 months → suspect neurologic pathology
  • Volkmann’s angle: maximum wrist extension with fingers extended = quantifies finger flexor contracture
  • Stereognosis: impaired in up to 97% of children

Assessment Tools

  • House Functional Use Scale: 0 (does not use) to 8 (spontaneous use)
  • MACS (Manual Abilities Classification System): Levels I-V
  • GMFCS (Gross Motor Function Classification System): Levels I-V
  • AHA (Assistive Hand Assessment): bimanual activity
  • Dynamic EMG: distinguishes spastic vs phasic muscle control → guides transfer decisions

Treatment

Non-operative

  • OT: splints, stretching, strengthening, functional use activities
  • Nighttime splinting for contractures; daytime for pre-positioning
  • Constraint therapy: higher-functioning children (forces affected hand use)
  • Tone management: diazepam, baclofen (oral/intrathecal pump), botulinum toxin, phenol, selective dorsal rhizotomy, selective neurectomy

Surgical Indications

  • Failed nonsurgical management with loss of ROM / developing contractures
  • Thresholds: elbow flexion contracture > 45°, pronation prevents supination past neutral, wrist flexion > 45°, fixed thumb adduction/MCP flexion
  • Ideal transfer candidate: ≥ 7 years old, full passive ROM, limited active ROM

Surgical Contraindications

  • Severe medical comorbidities
  • Dystonic or athetoid tone (poor candidates for lengthening/transfer)
  • Fixed joint contracture (contraindication to transfer alone)
  • Continuously spastic muscle (relative contraindication — may create opposite deformity)

Key Procedures

Elbow: Z-lengthening biceps + fascial lengthening brachialis; < 45° rarely needs surgery; > 90° approach cautiously (contracted NV bundle)

Forearm: PT release vs rerouting (dynamic EMG determines: spastic → release; phasic → transfer); FCU around ulnar border produces greatest supination (cadaveric); fixed supination more problematic than pronation in modern society

Wrist — FCU to ECRB Transfer: Harvest FCU at pisiform, mobilize distal 2/3 origin, subcutaneous tunnel dorsally, Pulvertaft weave to ECRB; tension so wrist rests in neutral against gravity

Wrist fusion: skeletally mature + severe contracture + little digital control; with PRC; test finger/thumb contractures pre/intra/postop

Fingers: Swan neck most common finger deformity (intrinsic spasticity + extrinsic extensor overactivity); Tonkin lateral band rerouting for locking > 40°; FDS tenodesis

Thumb-in-Palm — Four Types: - Type 1: adductor spasticity → 1st MC adduction - Type 2: + FPB → MCP flexion - Type 3: chronic adduction → secondary MCP hyperextension (do NOT augment EPB) - Type 4: + FPL → IP flexion

Matev release: release transverse head of adductor off 3rd MC; protect radial digital NV of long finger

EPL rerouting (Manske): EPL transected over MCP, retracted through 3rd compartment, rerouted through 1st compartment to achieve abduction

  • Dynamic EMG before surgery is invaluable — it tells you whether the pronator teres is truly spastic (release it) or has phasic control (transfer it); operating on a phasic muscle as though spastic can create the opposite deformity

Postoperative Protocol

  • Multiple simultaneous procedures common
  • Cast at 30° elbow flexion, maximum supination, slight wrist extension, thumb out of palm
  • Cast 4 weeks → custom splints + active ROM 3-5x/day → strengthening at 8 weeks

Complications & Outcomes

  • Tendon transfer > botulinum toxin or ongoing therapy at 12 months
  • Average improvement: 2.6 House functional levels
  • Cosmesis dramatically improved; patient satisfaction most highly correlated with cosmetic results
  • Hyperselective neurectomy (2/3 to 3/4 fascicles): for elbow flexor spasticity in patients with near-full passive ROM + volitional control + true spasticity

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Hand dominance before 18 months = red flag for neurologic diagnosis
  • Volkmann’s angle quantifies flexor contracture — essential for surgical planning
  • Dystonic/athetoid patients are poor surgical candidates — spasticity is the treatable pattern
  • Swan neck is the most common finger deformity in CP (not boutonniere)
  • Type 3 thumb-in-palm: do NOT augment EPB (MCP hyperextension is secondary to chronic adduction)
  • FCU to ECRB tension: wrist should rest in neutral against gravity — overtensioning creates extension contracture
  • Cosmesis drives satisfaction more than function in CP surgery

Board-Style Questions (4)

  1. Q: A 10-year-old with hemiplegic CP has wrist flexion contracture > 45° and dynamic EMG shows spastic FCU with phasic ECRB. What is the recommended procedure? — A: FCU to ECRB tendon transfer — the FCU is spastic (removing the deforming force) and the ECRB is phasic (can accept a transfer for wrist extension).
  2. Q: What is the most common finger deformity in cerebral palsy? — A: Swan neck deformity (from intrinsic spasticity and extrinsic extensor overactivity).
  3. Q: A child with CP has a type 3 thumb-in-palm deformity with MCP hyperextension. Why should EPB augmentation be avoided? — A: The MCP hyperextension is secondary to chronic adduction, not EPB weakness — augmenting EPB worsens the hyperextension deformity.
  4. Q: What tone pattern is a contraindication to tendon transfer in CP? — A: Dystonic or athetoid tone — these patterns are unpredictable and poor candidates for lengthening or transfer.
