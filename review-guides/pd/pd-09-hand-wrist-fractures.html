Pediatric Hand and Wrist Fractures

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Distal radius fractures: most common pediatric fractures (31% of all), 1.5x more common in boys
  • Physis is biomechanically weaker than ligaments → physeal injuries more common than ligamentous in children
  • Thicker periosteum = more inherently stable shaft fractures; greater remodeling potential
  • Remodeling occurs in the plane of motion; coronal and rotational deformities will NOT remodel
  • 5-10° of rotational malunion causes visible digital scissoring

Classification

Distal Phalanx Injuries

  • Seymour fracture: open SH I/II physeal injury; eponychium avulsed and entrapped in fracture → high osteomyelitis risk if missed; requires open debridement + nail bed repair + fixation
  • Mallet finger: extensor avulsion at distal phalanx epiphysis (tendon or SH III/IV bony); most managed with extension splinting 6 weeks regardless of fragment size
  • Jersey finger: zone I FPL avulsion; diagnose clinically; US useful

Proximal Phalanx Injuries

  • Unicondylar/bicondylar: inherently unstable even nondisplaced — follow weekly; limited blood supply through collateral origin; late osteotomy risks AVN
  • Subcapital (neck): distal fragment dorsally displaced blocking PIP flexion; remodeling very slow (years), only in plane of motion; closed reduction + percutaneous pinning: 92% good-excellent; percutaneous osteoclasis successful up to 36 days
  • Extra-octave fracture (base SH II): closed reduction with MCP flexion + buddy taping; broad fracture surface + physeal proximity = substantial remodeling

Metacarpal Injuries

  • Boxer’s fracture: unimpaired function with no grip strength loss even up to 75° flexion; soft wrapping alone similar to closed reduction and casting
  • Shaft fractures: malrotation will not remodel
  • Thumb MC base: tolerates up to 30° angulation

MCP Joint Injuries

  • Dorsal dislocation: irreducible “noose” — volar plate trapped between accessory collateral ligaments, flexors, lumbricals, natatory ligament; reduce via hyperextension then push base over MC head; dorsal open approach if irreducible
  • UCL avulsion (Skier’s thumb): high Stener lesion incidence → complete injuries best treated operatively; in children = SH III/IV pattern; fragment size often underestimated on XR

Workup

Imaging

  • Scaphoid fracture series: PA with maximal ulnar deviation; 81% occult fractures detected by MRI (NPV 100%)
  • Scaphoid fat pad sign may be present
  • Buckle fractures: repeat XR unnecessary (clinical healing assessment sufficient)

Treatment

Distal Radius Fractures

Type Management
Buckle (torus) Removable brace/wrap (superior to cast per RCTs); 3-4 weeks
Greenstick Well-molded cast ± closed reduction; weekly XR
Complete Weekly XR monitoring × 3 weeks; percutaneous pinning for instability
Bayonet apposition Children < 10: manage without formal reduction; remodels 6-9 months

Physeal Fractures (Distal Radius)

  • Most common: SH I/II; fracture through zone of provisional calcification
  • Gentle closed reduction — forceful or multiple attempts increase physeal arrest risk
  • No reductions after 10 days (increases arrest risk)
  • SH III/IV with > 2 mm articular displacement → surgery
  • Physeal arrest: 1-7% of all distal radius physeal injuries
  • Peterson type I (Mercedes fracture): metaphyseal fracture with vertical physeal component — not in Salter-Harris system

Scaphoid Fractures

  • Most common pediatric carpal fracture; peak age ~15; 28-37% may present as nonunions
  • Distribution (open physes): waist 67.9%, distal pole 26.5%, proximal pole 5.9%
  • Cast times: distal 4-8 weeks, waist 7-16 weeks, proximal ~15 weeks
  • Healing rates 90-99% when promptly diagnosed and treated
  • Displaced: retrograde headless screw (volar approach) for waist/distal; antegrade (dorsal) for proximal
  • AVN of proximal pole rare in pediatric patients (vs adults)

Galeazzi / DRUJ

  • 0.3-3% of pediatric forearm fractures; Galeazzi-equivalent = distal ulnar physeal injury
  • Anatomic radius reduction usually realigns ulna
  • Dorsal dislocation: immobilize in supination; volar: in pronation

TFCC Injuries

  • Usually associated with distal radius fractures; most heal with long arm cast 4-6 weeks

  • Persistent ulnar pain → MRI, arthroscopic repair/debridement

  • Pediatric boxer’s fractures tolerate up to ~75° flexion with unimpaired function and no grip strength loss; soft wrap is non-inferior to casting — don’t overtreat

  • Phalangeal neck fractures look benign on x-ray but are inherently unstable and remodel slowly — pin if displaced

Complications & Outcomes

  • Seymour fracture: high osteomyelitis rate if missed (initially appears as simple mallet)
  • Phalangeal condyle fractures: AVN risk with late open osteotomy → condylar advancement osteotomy for severe malunion
  • Distal radius physeal arrest: 1-7%; risk factors = forceful/late manipulation, SH III/IV, compression

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Seymour fracture is the most commonly missed pediatric hand injury — look for nail plate malalignment + eponychial swelling
  • Coronal and rotational deformity does NOT remodel — only sagittal plane remodeling occurs
  • Buckle fractures: removable brace, no repeat XR needed, clinical follow-up only
  • Boxer’s fractures in kids: up to 75° acceptable — don’t reduce, don’t cast
  • Phalangeal neck fractures: look benign but remodel poorly — pin if displaced
  • Unicondylar phalanx fractures: follow weekly even if nondisplaced — inherently unstable
  • Limit reduction attempts to 1-2 gentle manipulations; no reductions after 10 days

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A child’s nail plate is malaligned and swollen at the base after a crush injury. XR shows a SH I fracture of the distal phalanx. What is the diagnosis and management? — A: Seymour fracture (open physeal fracture with entrapped eponychium); requires open debridement, nail bed repair, and fracture stabilization.
  2. Q: A 7-year-old has a boxer’s fracture with 60° of flexion angulation. What is the recommended treatment? — A: Soft wrapping alone — up to 75° of angulation is acceptable with no loss of function or grip strength in children.
  3. Q: A distal radius fracture in a 12-year-old shows bayonet apposition on XR. The child is 9 years old. What management is appropriate? — A: Non-operative management (short arm cast); bayonet apposition in children < 10 remodels over 6-9 months.
  4. Q: A phalangeal neck fracture in a child appears minimally displaced on initial XR. What follow-up is recommended? — A: Weekly radiographic follow-up — phalangeal neck fractures are inherently unstable and remodel very slowly; early pinning if displacement develops.
  5. Q: What is the most common distribution of scaphoid fractures in children with open physes? — A: Waist (67.9%), followed by distal pole (26.5%) and proximal pole (5.9%).
