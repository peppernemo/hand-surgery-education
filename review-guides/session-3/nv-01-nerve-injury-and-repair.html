Nerve Injury and Repair

Anatomy / Basic Science

  • Layers (inside out): endoneurium (surrounds axon + Schwann cell basement membrane) → perineurium (blood-brain barrier extension; fascicles “pooch” when cut) → inner epineurium (cushion) → outer epineurium (vessels on surface = alignment landmarks)
  • Fascicular topography: Sunderland described intraneural plexus with branching/interconnections; more recent work shows functional segregation even proximally — critical for nerve transfer design
  • Blood supply: epineurial vessels → extensive internal network; nerve tolerates long-distance mobilization without ischemia (redundant supply reconstitutes in days)
  • Wallerian degeneration: initiates 24-48 hrs post-injury; Schwann cells transdifferentiate, form Bands of Bungner (guidance channels); mediators include IL-4, IL-10, NGF, CNTF, GDNF
  • Motor axons show slight preference for motor endoneurial tubes (limited specificity)
  • 1% of functional potential lost per week of denervation; after ~18 months, muscle irreversibly replaced by fibro-adipose tissue
  • Regeneration rate: 1 mm/day (1 inch/month, 1.5 ft/year)
  • Proximal injuries = worse prognosis (longer regeneration distance, axon “steal” by proximal muscles)

Classification

  • Sunderland (modified by MacKinnon):
    • I (neurapraxia): myelin/local environment only; recovers minutes-months
    • II (axonotmesis): axons disrupted, endoneurial tubes intact; spontaneous recovery
    • III: endoneurial tubes disrupted; variable recovery
    • IV: perineurium disrupted; no spontaneous recovery
    • V (neurotmesis): complete rupture; requires surgery
    • VI (MacKinnon): mixed pattern across zone of injury — hardest to manage

Workup

Exam

  • Serial exams (monthly): active ROM, isolated muscle strength, sensory testing, Tinel tracking
  • Progressing Tinel = controversial but useful if documented with focal locations + measurements
  • Neuroma pain (mechanical, triggered by direct stimulus/stretch) vs neuropathic pain (burning, tingling, nocturnal, cortical imprinting) — local block differentiates

Electrodiagnostics

  • Wait 2-3 weeks post-injury for meaningful NCS/EMG (Wallerian degeneration must progress)
  • Neurapraxia: intact conduction distal to injury, no fibrillations
  • Axonotmesis/neurotmesis: loss of distal conduction, fibrillations on EMG
  • Cannot reliably distinguish Sunderland II from III/IV early
  • 3-4 months: intraoperative nerve-to-nerve conduction across zone of injury; action potential present = 90% chance of continued recovery

Imaging

  • US or MR neurography to identify complete ruptures (surgical indication)
  • MRI advances may distinguish injury grades but clinical utility unproven

Treatment

Non-operative

  • Sunderland I-II with NCS/EMG confirmation or early clinical recovery: observation
  • Passive ROM to prevent joint stiffness; strengthening once any recovery detected
  • Gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline for neuropathic pain (minimize narcotics)
  • Mirror therapy + desensitization for neuroma/neuropathic pain

Operative

  • Indications: laceration with deficit, confirmed neurotmesis, closed injuries without recovery at 3-4 months

  • Repair ASAP to minimize axotomy/denervation effects + stump retraction

  • Avulsion/blast/GSW: wait 2-3 weeks for zone demarcation; acute repair of avulsed radial nerves = universal failure

  • Tension > 8-15% = decreased perfusion, impaired Schwann cell function, stunted regeneration

  • Coaptation tools:

    • Suture (epineurial > fascicular in most situations)
    • Fibrin glue (fast, equivalent in animal studies; risk of catastrophic failure; not FDA-approved for nerve)
    • Connectors (entubulation improves alignment, protects repair site, may auto-correct malalignment)

Gap Bridging

  • Conduits: for digital/sensory nerves, gaps < 2.5 cm; collagen, PGA, polycaprolactone; fibrin clot scaffold unreliable > 15 mm
  • Acellular nerve allograft (ANA): 3D neural architecture advantage; digital/sensory up to 5-7 cm; growing use in mixed nerves; 84% S3+/S4 for short sensory gaps; autograft still gold standard for gaps > 7 cm
  • Autograft: sural nerve = workhorse (30-40 cm per leg); cable graft technique for larger nerves; harvest 15-20% longer than defect; vascularized grafts theoretically better but not widely adopted
  • Nerve transfers: sacrifice redundant nerve for critical function; especially useful for proximal injuries, long gaps, hostile tissue beds

Post-op / Rehab

  • Protective immobilization 3 weeks post-coaptation (60% burst strength at 8 wk in rodents, 77% at 4 wk in primates)
  • Early motion OK after digital nerve repair (does not affect outcomes)
  • Protected early ROM takes precedence if concurrent flexor tendon repair
  • Emphasis on maintaining supple joints, pain control, nerve-modulating medications

Complications & Outcomes

  • Digital nerve primary repair: mean static 2PD ~10.6 mm (normal contralateral 4.4 mm); 48-72% good/excellent; sharp > crush; age matters; children approach 100% normal sensation
  • Digital nerve gaps ≤ 3 cm: autograft 70-100% S3+/S4; ANA 83-84% S3+/S4; conduits 50-89%
  • Mixed/motor nerves: primary repair 72% ≥ grade 3 (ulnar), 85% (median/radial); early repair > delayed
  • Autograft for mixed nerves: useful motor (M3+) in 63-81%; gaps > 4.5 cm (ulnar), 5 cm (median), 8 cm (radial) = worse
  • ANA for mixed: 77-81% meaningful recovery (M3+); only 40-50% achieve M4+
  • Sural nerve harvest: persistent sensory loss in 93%; allodynia in 50% (few consider it a major problem)
  • Neuroma management: reconstruction preferred when distal stump available; TMR (targeted muscle reinnervation) gaining enthusiasm; centro-central repair for digital stump neuromas

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Neurapraxia vs axonotmesis cannot be distinguished in first 2-3 weeks — early EDX has minimal value
  • Fascicles “pooch” from cut nerves due to perineurial pressure — inspect for this to confirm healthy fascicular pattern during resection
  • Epineurial vessels = key alignment landmarks during repair
  • Conduit gap > 15 mm for digital nerves = less predictable fibrin clot scaffold
  • Radial nerve has best prognosis; ulnar nerve (especially proximal) has worst
  • ANA vs autograft: unsettled debate; patient preference (cadaver tissue vs donor site morbidity) often decides

Board-Style Questions (3-5)

  1. Q: A patient sustains a closed humeral shaft fracture with complete radial nerve palsy. NCS/EMG at 3 weeks shows absent conduction distal to injury with no fibrillations. What does this suggest? — A: Too early to differentiate neurapraxia from axonotmesis; distal conduction may persist 3-11 days post-transection, and fibrillations take several weeks to develop. Repeat at 6 weeks.
  2. Q: What is the maximum gap length for which a synthetic conduit is generally reliable for digital nerve reconstruction? — A: < 2.5 cm (results decline significantly beyond 8-12 mm in some series).
  3. Q: A 30-year-old sustains a sharp laceration of the ulnar nerve at the wrist. Primary repair is performed. At what rate should you expect axon regeneration, and when might intrinsic function return? — A: 1 mm/day; ~8-10 months to hand intrinsics, though proximal ulnar injuries have a dismal intrinsic recovery prognosis regardless.
  4. Q: Intraoperative nerve conduction testing across a neuroma-in-continuity demonstrates an action potential. What is the recommended management? — A: Continued observation; presence of an action potential across the lesion is associated with a 90% chance of continued spontaneous recovery.
  5. Q: After 18 months of muscle denervation, why is nerve repair unlikely to restore meaningful motor function? — A: Schwann cell senescence, loss of endoneurial guidance channels, dispersion of ACh receptor clusters, satellite cell exhaustion, and irreversible fibro-adipose replacement of muscle.
