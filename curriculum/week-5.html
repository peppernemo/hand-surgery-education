Week 5 · Tendon

Resident track, week 5 of 7 · Intern track, week 4 of 4 (flexor portion)

Flexor tendon surgery has a narrow margin for technical error and lives or dies on rehab. Extensor injuries look simpler and are undertreated into bad outcomes. This week: zones, pulleys, Leddy, Doyle, mallet, and the modern early-active-motion mindset.

Self-test (attempt before opening the key)

  1. Name the annular and cruciate pulleys and the two that matter most biomechanically. How much venting is acceptable?
  2. Classify jersey finger (Leddy I-IV). Which type must be repaired within 7-10 days and why?
  3. Name the Doyle extensor zones and the classic injury at zones I, III, and V.
  4. What percent laceration of a flexor tendon warrants formal repair, and what is the trap with partial lacerations under A2?
  5. Describe the OR plan for a zone II flexor laceration: anesthesia, exposure, core and epitendinous technique, pulley management, splint position, and rehab start.
  6. A closed soft-tissue mallet with 25 degrees of lag: treatment, duration, and the operative indications for mallet injuries.
  7. Why does early active motion beat immobilization after a modern multi-strand repair?
Answer key
  1. A1-A5 and C1-C3. A2 (proximal phalanx) and A4 (middle phalanx) prevent bowstringing. Modern practice tolerates venting up to ~25% of A2 or all of A4 to let a repair glide.
  2. Leddy I: FDP retracts to the palm, both vincula torn, no nutrition, the muscle shortens: repair within 7-10 days. II: retracts to PIP (long vinculum intact), weeks of grace. III: bony fragment caught at A4. IV: fracture plus tendon avulsed off the fragment; fix both.
  3. Odd zones sit over joints: I DIP = mallet; III PIP = central slip (boutonniere; test with Elson); V MCP = sagittal band rupture (traumatic) or fight bite (assume the joint is violated until proven otherwise).
  4. Repair at > 50-60% laceration; smaller ones get debrided and mobilized. A partial cut under A2 can catch and trigger, so a “moving but painful” finger still needs a real exam and sometimes exploration.
  5. WALANT or block (WALANT lets you watch the repair glide with active motion), Bruner or midlateral extension, retrieve the tendon without grabbing it through the sheath, 4- or 6-strand core (3-0/4-0 braided) + running 5-0/6-0 epitendinous, vent pulleys as needed, dorsal blocking splint (wrist ~30 flexion, MCPs ~60, IPs straight), early active protocol with therapy starting day 3-5.
  6. Full-time DIP extension splinting 6-8 weeks then nighttime weaning; never let the tip drop. Operative indications: bony mallet with volar subluxation of the distal phalanx (the subluxation, not the fragment size, is Dr. Barrera’s indication), chronic symptomatic mallet, failed splinting.
  7. Multi-strand repairs tolerate load; motion drives intrinsic healing and glide, preventing the adhesions that immobilization guarantees. The repair you cannot rehab is a repair that fails functionally.

Run the check-in

Cases to walk through:

  1. ★ A 22-year-old football player grabbed a jersey; he cannot flex the ring-finger DIP and there is a tender mass in the palm. Diagnosis with Leddy type, the timeline conversation, and your OR plan including how you retrieve and fix the tendon.
  2. A 35-year-old chef cut the middle finger over the proximal phalanx 6 hours ago and cannot flex either joint. Walk from the ED exam (what confirms both tendons are cut) through your zone II repair and the exact splint and therapy prescription he leaves with.
  3. A 40-year-old jammed his finger a month ago and now has a droopy DIP; a fresh film shows a dorsal base fragment with the distal phalanx subluxated volarly. What changed the plan, and what do you offer?
