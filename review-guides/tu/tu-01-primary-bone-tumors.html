Primary Bone Tumors of the Hand and Wrist

Workup

History + Exam

  • Pain, growth pattern, occupation, family history
  • Size, mobility, location, firmness; adjacent joint ROM; skin changes, venous engorgement
  • Neuro exam (compression from soft tissue extension); SWM (2.83 = normal); pulses (accentuated → malignancy proximity)
  • Labs: CBC, chemistry, LFTs, UA; SPEP/UPEP for lytic lesions

Imaging

  • Plain XR always — whole bone, joints above + below
  • Cortical breakthrough = malignant or aggressive benign (GCT)
  • 50% cortex destruction → pathologic fracture risk (midshaft loses 90% strength)
  • Local CT: 1 mm cuts (larger cuts miss cortical breakthrough)
  • MRI: soft tissue extension; T2 enhancement (also blood/pus); fat bright on T1 + T2
  • Staging (if malignancy/GCT suspected): CT chest/abd/pelvis, bone scan or PET

Biopsy

  • Most important step — errors lower when done at definitive treating institution
  • Open biopsy: GA (not regional — tumor spread risk), no exsanguination (elevate 3-5 min), tourniquet 250 mmHg, small longitudinal incision, place where excisable at definitive op
  • Frozen section for non-osseous only — bone needs decalcification (days)
  • Core needle: ~90% accuracy, less contamination; risk = sampling error

Barrera: For tumor cases in the OR, skip Esmarch exsanguination — elevate only. Standard sequence: antibiotics → tourniquet placed → prep/drape → local → elevate + inflate (no Esmarch).

Classification

Enneking Staging:

Stage Grade Site Mets
IA Low (G1) Intracompartmental (T1) M0
IB Low Extracompartmental (T2) M0
IIA High (G2) T1 M0
IIB High T2 M0
III Any Any M1

Surgical Margins: Intralesional (through tumor) → Marginal (reactive zone) → Wide (normal tissue cuff) → Radical (entire compartment)

Benign Tumors

Enchondroma — Most Common Bone Tumor of Hand

  • 70% of hand bone lesions; proximal phalanx > MC > middle phalanx
  • Asymptomatic; presents with pathologic fracture or incidental finding
  • XR: medullary, sharp sclerotic margins, expansile, punctate calcification
  • Growth after skeletal maturity = malignant transformation concern
  • Solitary → chondrosarcoma: ~1% risk
  • Ollier’s disease (multiple, non-hereditary): ~30% malignant transformation → annual surveillance
  • Maffucci’s syndrome (enchondromas + hemangiomas with phleboliths): 50-100% transformation
  • Treatment: observe if asymptomatic; acute fracture → heal fracture first then curettage + graft
  • Curettage + autograft: 4.5% recurrence; allograft comparable; Ca phosphate cement biomechanically superior

Giant Cell Tumor of Bone

  • 3rd-4th decade; 85% after age 20; hand 2%, distal radius 14% (3rd most common GCT site)
  • Distal radius = most common primary site for GCT metastatic to lung
  • Treat as malignancy — chest CT, bone scan/PET, local MRI + CT
  • Campanacci: I (contained) → II (cortical expansion) → III (cortical perforation)
  • Curettage alone: 49-79% recurrence — NOT recommended
  • Adjuvants (PMMA, phenol, cryo, argon, ethanol) ↓ recurrence ≤ 27%; PMMA reduces recurrence 8×
  • Campanacci III → wide excision + reconstruction (vascularized fibula if > 6 cm)
  • Pronator quadratus = volar tumor barrier at distal radius
  • Bisphosphonates + denosumab = potential medical adjuvants

Aneurysmal Bone Cyst

  • 80% in 1st 2 decades; metacarpals + proximal phalanges; USP6 translocation
  • XR: expansile, sclerotic border, bony septae; MRI fluid/fluid levels = classic
  • Marginal curettage + bone graft; high recurrence; cryosurgery/argon beam ↓ recurrence
  • Avoid RT (radiation-induced sarcoma risk)

Osteoid Osteoma

  • Only 2% of OO in hand/wrist → frequently missed (avg 13.5 mo diagnostic delay)
  • NSAID-responsive night pain; proximal phalanx most common in hand
  • Most common primary bone tumor of carpus (scaphoid = most common carpal location)
  • CT: pathognomonic round lucent nidus + sclerotic rim (25% missed on plain XR)
  • Excision preferred; RFA suboptimal in hand (NV thermal injury risk)
  • Osteoblastoma = nidus > 1.5 cm, histologically identical

Other Benign

  • Osteochondroma: cortical continuity with native bone (pathognomonic); no malignant degeneration in hand; simple excision. Masada classification for forearm deformity in MHO (I = ulnar OC + shortening; II = radial OC + radial head dislocation; III = distal radius OC)
  • BPOP / Nora’s lesion: no medullary continuity (vs osteochondroma) — key distinguishing feature
  • Fibrous dysplasia: GNAS mutation; ground-glass with sclerotic margins; nonoperative + bisphosphonates
  • Chondroblastoma: epiphyseal, chicken wire calcification; curettage + graft

Malignant Tumors

Chondrosarcoma — Most Common Hand Malignancy

  • 5th-6th decade; 26% diagnosed > 10 years after onset
  • XR: lytic, cortical destruction, stippled calcification, indistinct borders
  • Radioresistant + chemoresistant → surgery only
  • High-grade → wide en bloc resection / ray amputation
  • Low-grade (atypical cartilaginous tumor): curettage + cryo may preserve function — Meftah: 90.7% recurrence-free at 25 years

Osteosarcoma — 2nd Most Common Hand Malignancy

  • Hand cases present later in life; frequently low-grade; better survival than other sites
  • XR: lytic/blastic/mixed; Codman triangle periosteal reaction
  • Wide excision (often amputation) + adjuvant chemo (MTX, doxorubicin, ifosfamide, cisplatin)
  • Tumor necrosis > 90% post-neoadjuvant = favorable

Ewing’s Sarcoma

  • t(11;22) EWS-FLI-1 fusion; < 1% in hand; 1st-2nd decade
  • Fever, anemia, leukocytosis — misdiagnosed as infection
  • Histology: round cell, PAS-positive (glycogen)
  • Wide excision + neoadjuvant chemo; 5-yr DFS > 70%

Metastatic to Hand

  • Rare; lung > kidney > head/neck (lung > 40%); metacarpals ~50% of hand mets
  • Median survival ≤ 6 months; treatment = pain + function (ray/transverse amputation)

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Enchondroma is the most common hand bone tumor; chondrosarcoma is the most common hand bone malignancy
  • GCT at distal radius: treat as malignancy; curettage alone = unacceptable recurrence; add PMMA
  • Osteoid osteoma: frequently missed in hand — think of it with NSAID-responsive night pain in a young patient
  • BPOP vs osteochondroma: look for medullary continuity (present in OC, absent in BPOP)
  • Biopsy at the treating institution — Mankin showed ↑ complications at referring centers
  • Ray amputation = workhorse for hand sarcomas

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A 35-year-old woman presents with an expansile lytic lesion of the distal radius with cortical thinning. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what imaging workup is required? — A: Giant cell tumor; treat as malignancy with chest CT, bone scan or PET, and local MRI + CT.

  2. Q: A patient with multiple enchondromas and soft tissue hemangiomas with phleboliths on X-ray has what syndrome, and what is the risk of malignant transformation? — A: Maffucci’s syndrome; 50-100% malignant transformation risk requiring lifelong surveillance.

  3. Q: A 22-year-old presents with a proximal phalanx enchondroma that fractures through the lesion. Should you perform curettage and grafting acutely? — A: No — heal the fracture first, then perform curettage + bone graft. Early treatment during fracture healing increases complication rates.

  4. Q: What distinguishes BPOP (Nora’s lesion) from osteochondroma on imaging? — A: BPOP lacks medullary continuity with the underlying bone, whereas osteochondroma has cortical and medullary continuity with native bone.

  5. Q: What is the most common primary bone tumor of the carpus, and where does it most often occur? — A: Osteoid osteoma; scaphoid is the most common carpal location.
