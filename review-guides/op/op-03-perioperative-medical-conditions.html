Perioperative Medical Conditions in Hand Surgery

Treatment

Anticoagulation

  • Most hand/wrist procedures have minimal muscle dissection → low bleeding risk
  • Recommend continuing warfarin perioperatively unless high risk for hematoma necrosis or compartment syndrome
  • Continued antiplatelets distal to DR metaphysis: rare bleeding complications
  • ASA discontinuation for secondary coronary prevention: up to 3x increased cardiac complications; thrombotic events average 11 days post-cessation
  • Rebound prothrombotic state with acute withdrawal of antiplatelets or warfarin
  • VKA stoppage (when indicated): stop 5 days preop, resume 12-24h postop
  • Bridging anticoagulation: yes for high VTE risk, no for low risk; meta-analysis shows bridging increases bleeding without reducing VTE
  • NOACs: low bleeding risk surgery = no interruption (ASRA: ≥ 12h last dose to surgery); no bridging if interruption < 96h
  • NOAC reversal: idarucizumab (dabigatran-specific); activated charcoal within 2h for dabigatran/apixaban; hemodialysis decreases dabigatran

VTE Prophylaxis in UE Surgery

  • No DVT reported after elbow/wrist/hand surgery (shoulder arthroplasty has documented DVT/PE)
  • PE rate as high as 36% with UE DVT when it occurs
  • No routine chemical prophylaxis recommended (US + UK guidelines)
  • Mechanical prophylaxis recommended for all elective UE surgery (low cost/risk)
  • NOACs not FDA-approved for UE surgery VTE prophylaxis

Diabetes Mellitus

  • “Diabetic hand”: limited joint mobility, Dupuytren’s (2-8x more common), trigger finger (up to 20% prevalence), CTS (up to 15%)

  • Trigger finger in DM: more likely bilateral, multidigit, fail CSI → require surgery; insulin-dependent = higher failure rate

  • Blood glucose elevation post-CSI lasts up to 5 days — counsel diabetic patients

  • CTS in DM: more advanced nerve pathology on NCS but no clinical outcome difference after OCTR vs non-diabetic

  • Ideal HbA1c < 7.5% preop, optimal ≤ 7.0%

  • Counsel diabetic patients that steroid injections are less likely to work and blood glucose will spike for up to 5 days. For trigger finger in insulin-dependent diabetics with multiple digits involved, consider early surgical release rather than repeated injections.

Perioperative DM Medication Management

  • Hold day of surgery: sulfonylureas, alpha-glucose inhibitors, short/rapid-acting insulin
  • Hold day before + day of: SGLT-2 inhibitors
  • Continue: metformin (hold for IV contrast), thiazolidinediones, DPP-4 inhibitors
  • Insulin adjustments: intermediate-acting = 50% morning dose day of; long-acting = 75% morning dose; pump = sick day/sleep basal rate

Rheumatoid Arthritis

  • Preop cervical spine evaluation essential: 43-86% have cervical involvement; up to 50% with radiographic disease are asymptomatic
  • Recommend flexion-extension cervical XR minimum + communicate with anesthesia
  • Atlantoaxial instability, cranial settling, subaxial subluxation → affects intubation
  • DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, HCQ): generally safe to continue perioperatively
  • Biologics (etanercept, infliximab, abatacept): stop, schedule surgery at end of dosing cycle, resume ≥ 14 days postop if no wound issues
  • Decreasing rate of rheumatoid hand surgery with modern DMARDs/biologics

NSAIDs and Bone Healing

  • Short-term use (< 3-4 weeks): no impact on bone healing
  • Long-term (> 3-4 weeks): association with nonunion and delayed union
  • COX-2 inhibition decreases PGE2 required for BMP-induced osteogenesis

CKD

  • 14% of US adults; CKD = risk factor for postop AKI, MI, heart failure, stroke
  • Platelet dysfunction → bleeding tendency; also prothrombotic
  • Dialysis day before surgery preferred; emergent = same day without heparin
  • Pain management: avoid NSAIDs (nephrotoxic if CrCl < 30), avoid morphine + codeine; prefer tramadol, fentanyl

Hepatic Disease

  • Risk assessment: Child-Pugh + MELD score
  • Elective surgery contraindicated in acute hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, acute liver failure, decompensated cirrhosis
  • Acetaminophen safe and first-line for pain; avoid NSAIDs
  • Opioids: avoid controlled-release; co-prescribe laxatives (prevent constipation + encephalopathy)

Smoking

  • Independent predictor of unplanned readmission after outpatient hand/elbow surgery
  • Increased infection after trigger digit release and open CTR
  • Increased CTS development + more severe preop symptoms + persistent symptoms after OCTR
  • Cessation ≥ 4 weeks preop for best outcomes
  • E-cigarettes have equivalent complication rate and microcirculation impact

Sleep Apnea

  • 80-90% undiagnosed; STOP-BANG screening tool
  • Snoring, Tired, Observed apnea, blood Pressure, BMI > 35, Age > 50, Neck circumference (M ≥ 17”, F ≥ 16”), Gender male
  • Low: 0-2; Intermediate: 3-4; High: 5-8
  • Suspected/undiagnosed OSA → worse outcomes than preop diagnosis (↑ respiratory complications, ICU admission, 30-day mortality)
  • WALANT ideal for OSA — patient protects own airway
  • Restart CPAP at outpatient settings ASAP post-extubation; minimize postop opioids

Pregnancy

  • Imaging: maximize US + MRI; minimize ionizing radiation gestational weeks 3-8 (organogenesis)

  • 1st/2nd gen cephalosporins: Category B (relatively safe); aminoglycosides + vancomycin: Category C

  • Warfarin contraindicated in pregnancy

  • Ibuprofen/ketorolac: Category D in 3rd trimester

  • CTS in pregnancy: prevalence 1-62%, often bilateral, 3rd trimester; > 90% resolve within 2 weeks of delivery; night splinting gives > 80% relief; surgery generally avoided

  • CTS in pregnancy is driven by fluid retention, not structural compression — reassure patients that the vast majority resolve after delivery. Night splinting is first-line; surgery is rarely indicated.

Amyloidosis

  • Misfolded transthyretin (TTR) deposition in carpal tenosynovium + TCL
  • Consider tenosynovial biopsy with Congo red staining during CTR for: men ≥ 50, women ≥ 60, bilateral CTS, history of spinal stenosis or biceps tendon rupture, atrial fibrillation, CHF, family history

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • Default to continuing anticoagulation for hand/wrist surgery — the bleeding risk is almost always lower than the thromboembolic risk of stopping
  • No DVT has been reported after elbow/wrist/hand surgery — do not over-prophylax
  • RA patients need cervical spine imaging before intubation even if asymptomatic — up to 50% have silent disease
  • Continue DMARDs perioperatively; hold biologics and schedule surgery at end of dosing cycle
  • Counsel smokers that vaping is not safer — equivalent complication rate
  • STOP-BANG: undiagnosed OSA is more dangerous than diagnosed OSA perioperatively
  • Short-course NSAIDs (< 3-4 weeks) do not impair fracture healing

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A patient on warfarin for atrial fibrillation needs open CTR. Should warfarin be held? — A: No. Hand/wrist procedures are low bleeding risk; continuing warfarin is recommended to avoid thromboembolic complications.
  2. Q: An insulin-dependent diabetic has bilateral trigger fingers involving 3 digits. CSI has failed once per digit. What is the next step? — A: Surgical release. Insulin-dependent DM has higher CSI failure rates and multi-digit involvement predicts poor injection response.
  3. Q: A 55-year-old man with bilateral CTS, spinal stenosis, and atrial fibrillation undergoes CTR. What additional step should be considered? — A: Tenosynovial biopsy with Congo red staining to rule out ATTR amyloidosis.
  4. Q: What preoperative evaluation is essential before GA in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis? — A: Flexion-extension cervical spine radiographs — 43-86% have cervical involvement, and up to 50% are asymptomatic.
  5. Q: A pregnant patient in the third trimester presents with bilateral CTS. What is the recommended management? — A: Night splinting (> 80% relief); surgery is generally avoided as > 90% resolve within 2 weeks of delivery.
