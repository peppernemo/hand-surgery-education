Tendinopathies of the Forearm, Wrist, and Hand

Basic Science

  • Tendinosis = chronic degenerative change; pulley/sheath thickening → size mismatch → restricted gliding
  • Often gradual onset preceded by increased activity/use; no clear causal work-tendinopathy link established
  • May coexist with RA, crystalline arthropathy, autoimmune disease

De Quervain’s Tenosynovitis (1st Compartment)

Anatomy

  • APL (often multiple slips) + EPB; EPB in separate subcompartment 35-45%
  • Radial sensory nerve branches lie superficial to retinaculum

Workup

  • Most common in caregivers of young children + repetitive radial/ulnar deviation
  • Risk factors: pregnancy, age > 40, female, Black race
  • Finkelstein’s test: pain with active radial → ulnar deviation while grasping flexed thumb
  • EPB entrapment test: thumb MCP extension vs palmar abduction — if extension MORE painful → suggests separate EPB subcompartment

Treatment

Non-operative: - Forearm-based thumb spica × 4-6 weeks - CSI: 70-100% efficacy (high failure rate if EPB subcompartment or triggering) - Splinting alone doesn’t add to injection success

Barrera: CSI = Kenalog 10 mg/mL + 1% lidocaine (never Depo-Medrol). For de Quervain’s, approximately 2 mL total volume into the first dorsal compartment.

Surgical: - Longitudinal incision; protect radial sensory nerve branches - Complete retinacular division along midline or slightly dorsal (minimize volar subluxation) - Always search for and release EPB subcompartment - 100% resolution at 16-year follow-up (Scheller, 100 patients)

Complications

  • Radial sensory nerve injury (usually neuropraxic), incomplete release (missed EPB subcompartment), volar tendon subluxation, painful neuroma

Intersection Syndrome (2nd Compartment)

  • 1st + 2nd compartment tendons cross ~7 cm proximal to wrist
  • Classic in rowers; swelling + crepitus over dorsal radial distal forearm
  • Nonoperative usually successful: rest, NSAIDs, splint (15-30° wrist extension), CSI at intersection site
  • Surgery rare: retinacular release of 2nd compartment

EPL Tenosynovitis / Rupture (3rd Compartment)

  • Extremely rare except in RA or after non-displaced DRF (attritional from ischemia + hematoma at Lister’s tubercle)
  • Prodrome: pain over Lister + triggering on thumb extension
  • Rupture → loss of thumb IP extension + retropulsion
  • Prophylactic decompression if impending; EIP-to-EPL transfer after rupture

4th Compartment (EDC + EIP)

  • More common in RA/inflammatory arthropathy
  • Tuck sign: tenosynovitis mass catches in retinaculum during excursion
  • EIP syndrome: distal muscle belly trapped in retinaculum → triggering with active index extension
  • Vaughan-Jackson syndrome: DRUJ arthritis → attritional ruptures ulnar → radial (EDQ → EDC small → ring…)

ECU Tendinopathy / Instability (6th Compartment)

  • Two categories: tenosynovitis vs instability/subluxation (can coexist)
  • Painful snapping with supination; pain with resisted ulnar deviation in pronation
  • ECU synergy test: differentiates from intra-articular pathology
  • Subsheath tears may accompany TFCC tears
  • Nonoperative: rest, splint, CSI; instability → pronation + radial deviation splint × 4 weeks
  • Surgical: subsheath release (no instability) or retinacular flap reconstruction (with instability)

Stenosing Tenosynovitis (Trigger Finger)

Workup

  • Prevalence up to 3% general, higher in diabetics; thumb + ring most common; middle-aged females
  • A1 pulley enlarges up to 3× normal + intratendinous nodule
  • Associated: CTS, de Quervain’s, Dupuytren’s, RA
  • 4-grade classification: I (pre-triggering) → II (active, self-reduced) → III (passive reduction) → IV (fixed PIP contracture)

Non-operative

  • Oral NSAIDs alone NOT effective
  • Orthosis: 47-93% success (no consensus on design)
  • CSI: 60-90% long-term relief in up to 3 injections; worst outcomes with DM, prolonged/severe duration
  • Complications: skin depigmentation, fat necrosis, tendon weakening, elevated blood glucose in DM

Barrera: CSI = Kenalog 10 mg/mL + 1% lidocaine, ~1 mL total volume for trigger finger A1 pulley injection.

Operative (Open A1 Release — Gold Standard)

  • Protect NV bundles; thumb: radial digital nerve crosses A1 ulnar → radial (HIGH iatrogenic injury risk)
  • Release superficial palmar aponeurosis if residual triggering
  • Percutaneous: long + ring fingers best candidates (safest NV anatomy)
  • A1 + up to 50% proximal A2 release safe biomechanically; > 50% A2 → pulley reconstruction
  • Near-perfect outcomes with open release; minimal recurrence

FCR Tendinitis

  • FCR in fibro-osseous tunnel adjacent to trapezium; associated with STT arthritis, thumb CMC arthritis, CPPD
  • Tenderness at FCR before trapezial entry = highly indicative
  • Nonoperative: NSAIDs, splint, CSI (caution: may weaken tendon → rupture)
  • Surgical: FCR decompression; doesn’t address concomitant STT or CMC pathology
  • Gabel: 90% excellent relief at 44 months

FCU Tendinitis / Calcific Tendinitis

  • Repetitive overuse (golfers, racquet sports); ulnar-sided volar forearm pain
  • Calcific tendinitis: rare; FCU most affected — dramatic presentation mimics infection; oblique XR shows pathognomonic calcium
  • Calcific FCU tendinitis is self-limited

Pearls / Pitfalls

  • De Quervain’s is the most common UE tendinopathy — always search for EPB subcompartment
  • Incomplete de Quervain’s release = missed EPB subcompartment
  • Vaughan-Jackson: ulnar-to-radial attritional ruptures from DRUJ pathology — think RA
  • Thumb A1 release: radial digital nerve at highest iatrogenic risk
  • Trigger finger: A1 + partial A2 release safe; > 50% A2 requires reconstruction
  • FCR tendinitis often masks underlying STT arthritis
  • Calcific FCU tendinitis is self-limited — aggressive surgery not needed

Board-Style Questions (5)

  1. Q: A patient has a de Quervain’s injection that fails. What anatomic variant should be suspected, and how is it identified intraoperatively? — A: Separate EPB subcompartment (present 35-45%); must be identified and released as a separate compartment during surgery.

  2. Q: What is the Vaughan-Jackson syndrome, and in what direction do ruptures progress? — A: Attritional extensor tendon ruptures from DRUJ arthritis/subluxation, progressing ulnar to radial (EDQ → EDC small → ring → middle).

  3. Q: During open A1 pulley release for thumb trigger finger, what structure is at highest risk of iatrogenic injury? — A: The radial digital nerve, which crosses the A1 pulley from ulnar to radial.

  4. Q: How much of the A2 pulley can be safely released in conjunction with A1 release for trigger finger? — A: Up to 50% of the proximal A2; beyond that risks bowstringing and requires pulley reconstruction.

  5. Q: A patient with RA presents with progressive inability to extend the small and ring fingers. What diagnosis should be considered, and what is the underlying mechanism? — A: Vaughan-Jackson syndrome — attritional rupture of EDQ then EDC tendons from DRUJ synovitis/dorsal ulnar head subluxation.
